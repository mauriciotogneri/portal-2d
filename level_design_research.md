# Level Design Research

Outside research for designing Portal 2D's campaign chambers, gathered in November 2026: how the makers of similar games designed their levels, what generic puzzle-design writing says, a catalog of patterns translated into Portal 2D's elements, and chamber ideas. It is reference material, not rules: the rules are `docs/elements.md`, and the project's own guidelines are `docs/level_design.md`. Where a finding matches or adds to one of those guidelines, it says so (guideline N).

Every claim links its source. Sources are primary where possible (developer commentary, talks, postmortems, designer interviews), then analysts (Game Maker's Toolkit, Game Developer, Electron Dance), then community (forums, wikis). Quotes from videos come from YouTube's automatic captions, so small words may differ. Mappings to Portal 2D are this document's own reading of `docs/elements.md`, not claims by the sources.

## The short version

1. **Start every chamber from one sentence**: the one moment it's about. Then build the smallest room where that moment is the only way through, and add one restriction per shortcut you find, noting what each blocks (Brett Taylor, Alan Hazelden, Arvi Teikari, Croteam, Valve; Part 3.1).
2. **Delete everything the solution doesn't mention.** Clutter confuses, adds busywork to every restart, and, the research shows, creates shortcuts rather than difficulty (Taylor's noise test; Refraction research; Part 3.2).
3. **Difficulty comes from tangled subgoals, not size.** In a study of Sokoban players, state-space size didn't predict solving time at all; whether the problem split into independent parts predicted it best. Two near-identical levels took 3 and 54 minutes (Jarušek and Pelánek; Part 3.4).
4. **Lead the player into a wrong assumption, let the catch be felt, and resolve it with a true consequence of the rules.** That's the shape of the best-remembered puzzles (Mark Brown's assumption → catch → revelation; Part 3.5).
5. **Teach each element alone, test it, twist it, combine it, and pace as a sawtooth**: complexity drops with each new element and climbs again. Measured in Portal, Portal 2, Braid and Lemmings; Talos 2 switched to it after Talos 1's steady ramp exhausted players (guidelines 40–41 confirmed; Part 3.4).
6. **A hard chamber creates the easy ones it needs.** When testers stall, insert a bridging chamber or change the chambers before it; don't add hints (Valve, Croteam, Patrick Traynor; Part 3.4).
7. **Portal 2D has no undo, so every dead end must be impossible or obvious the moment it happens**, and chambers short enough that a restart costs little (Sokobond, Talos 2, Chip's Challenge; Part 3.2).
8. **Make the goal obvious and the solution hidden.** "The player shouldn't be figuring out what to do - just how to do it" (Brown). Show the exit and what holds it shut from the start.
9. **Nothing decorative should look usable, and everything usable should stand out.** Players treat every visible thing as part of the puzzle, and animated things (glows, fields, flicker) draw the eye first (Valve, Blow, Grant; Part 3.8).
10. **Watch silently; a first-time player can be first only once.** About five testers per round, fresh eyes rationed per chamber, behaviour over opinions (Valve, Traynor, Nielsen; Part 3.7).
11. **Prove the negative by machine.** Your recorded solutions, replay check and solver already go beyond anything Valve used; the research adds concept-required checks, near-miss lists and one-change sweeps (Part 3.7).
12. **Three Portal 2D-specific holes**: shots pass open doors (Portal's TC04 cheat), a working walk is always taken because there's no time cost, and Portal 2D has no AND gate, so outside patterns with several inputs on one door need rebuilding (Parts 3.2, 4).
13. **Overproduce and cut.** The designers studied shipped a third to a half of what they made. For the 10 chambers still to make, budget 20–30 drafts (Part 3.1).
14. **The finale needn't be the hardest chamber.** Portal's most climactic moment was "about the easiest puzzle in Portal"; Portal 2D has no time pressure, so its last chamber needs synthesis and spectacle (Part 3.4).
15. **No chamber uses a light barrier or a mirror box yet**; Part 5 has teach chambers for both, and 31 ideas in all.

Points marked **[new]** in Part 3 that are concrete enough to become guidelines in `docs/level_design.md`. Nothing here has been added to the guide.

|---|---|---|
| 1 | Write the chamber's one-sentence idea before placing tiles; after building, delete every tile and element the solution doesn't mention, unless it stops a shortcut | Taylor, Martin, Croteam (3.1) |
| 2 | Keep a list of each restriction and what it blocks; one that blocks nothing is a red herring | Teikari (3.1) |
| 3 | A box-and-door chamber must decide whether "stand on the button, shoot through the open door" is the lesson or a cheat, and break the line if it's a cheat | Portal TC04, Flash Task 20 (3.2) |

## Part 2 — How each game's levels were designed

Grouped by family. Each entry: what the game is, how its levels were made (process, principles, decisions, short quotes), what to borrow for Portal 2D, and what doesn't carry over.


### Portal 2 (Valve, 2011), its co-op campaign and Puzzle Maker

**A naming trap first.** Portal 2's "laser" (the Thermal Discouragement Beam that powers catchers) is Portal 2D's **emitter and ray**; its laser catcher is the **receiver** and its reflection cube the **mirror box** (aimed freely there, a fixed 45° mirror here). Portal 2D's **laser** (blocks walking, not shots) is closer to Portal 2's deadly Laser Field. The Hard Light Bridge is mostly a floor there; Portal 2D's light barrier is a switchable wall. Portal 2's indicator lights are Portal 2D's antlines. Portal 2's laser relays (a beam lighting several nodes in a row) have no counterpart: a Portal 2D receiver ends the ray.

**How the levels were made.**
- *Goal first.* Each chamber starts from one teaching goal, sketched as an isometric layout on a whiteboard: "We never sit down and throw random pieces at the whiteboard to see what sticks" ([Game Informer 2010](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber.aspx)).
- *Two kinds of training per element.* "Checklisting": list the rules a new element imposes and give a "very clean room to safely experiment with it". Then a second, unnamed kind: list what the element can do and build lateral challenges that turn a learned rule on its head, "often the most rewarding for players" (same article).
- *A pool, then a list.* Phil Co: "we built test chambers based on mechanics first", ordered by "a simple text file" so the whole game could be tested in any order ([Interlopers interview](https://www.valvearchive.com/archive/Other%20Files/Interviews/Text/Interlopers/2012%20Phil%20Co%20Interview/Phil_Co.txt)). Puzzles stayed bare until stable; art came last, and every map was retested after art.
- *Tentpole first.* Matt T. Wood: ask "what is my ultimate challenge for the player", build it, find the "three or four things in here which the players don't really understand", then design the chambers before it that plant them ([Kiwi Talkz](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3dYAZxr2FbY)). Adam Foster on his map *Triple Axis*: "Every other gameplay element in the map is simply to support that moment" ([Radiator Blog interview](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/p/level-with-me-adam-foster-part-2.html)).
- *Fix a hard chamber by changing the ones before it.* Foster: "The usual route when dealing with a puzzle that seems too complicated is not to change it directly, but modify previous puzzles."
- *Execution is not difficulty.* Steam data showed Portal 1 quitters "almost universally" quit after one of its two hardest-to-execute puzzles, so Portal 2 moved difficulty into thinking ([Engadget, Wolpaw](https://www.engadget.com/2011-03-13-valves-erik-wolpaw-on-portal-2s-difficulty-ps3-steam-features.html)). Timing puzzles "regularly" frustrate players (Jeep Barnett, [Animex 2012](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VT1Eoq38H-k)).
- *Never test what you haven't taught.* Wolpaw: "At no point are we going to ask you do something that we haven't prepared you to do." The map names show the cadence: Laser Intro → Laser Stairs → Dual Lasers → Laser Over Goo; Turret Intro → Laser Relays → … → Laser Chaining → Triple Laser ([map list](https://wiki.portal2.sr/List_of_Singleplayer_maps)).
- *The laser run.* Laser Intro: one portal where the beam hits, one facing the catcher. Laser Stairs: a reflection cube turns the beam into the catcher, then the same cube is needed on a button. Dual Lasers: two beams, one cube; the cube solves one, portals the other. Laser Over Goo flips the lesson: blocking the beam is what helps ([Portal Wiki chamber pages](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_2_Chapter_2_Test_Chamber_1)). Lasers replaced Portal 1's energy pellets because they "stayed in one place and always let the player know how they will affect the environment" ([GDC 2012 report](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-i-portal-2-i-making-a-sequel-to-a-perfect-game)).
- *What the developer commentary records* ([Combine OverWiki](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)):
  - Fizzler Intro: two fizzlers and three areas meant "almost none of our playtesters could solve the puzzle". Even simplified, players "could not figure out which side of the fizzler they needed to be on" until one gap at eye level, valid from either side, gave a single clear sightline.
  - Testers couldn't describe fizzlers they had walked through in almost every map, so shots now flash on them.
  - An extra puzzle hidden beyond the reflection cube "took players so long to get to it, that they were confused about which elements were still relevant": the space was condensed and the exit put near the start.
  - Testers fixated on the funnel, "forgetting that they had a portal gun", so an event before the chamber forces the same portal move in another context.
  - "Elevator fatigue": too many complex chambers in a row tired testers, so a ride became a rest.
  - Co-op's *Bonk Heads*: a puzzle just before it "where two cubes repeatedly collide" cut the solve time but "almost completely robbed the appeal". Planting fragments four maps earlier and framing the path with the room's layout let players "own that exciting dual collision epiphany".
  - When players could wander between areas, "they logically assumed that the individual puzzles were part of one big puzzle", so airlocks separate them.
  - Long death animations made players "afraid to take risks", so respawn became instant.
  - Co-op's hub, where players picked any chamber, "prevented us from doing sufficient training"; going linear "could guarantee prior knowledge".
- *Small changes, big swings.* Triple Laser had players "permanently stuck" until "some incredibly subtle changes in the cubes' initial positions" fixed it: "We don't really know why" (Foster). Start positions are a difficulty dial.
- *Done when* most testers find it fun and get through "without getting irretrievably stuck"; "it's absolutely fine to take a while", "Getting the puzzle immediately is bad, but it still has to be solveable" (Foster).
- *Co-op uniqueness rule.* "If one person can figure out how to solve it with just one set of portals then it's back to the drawing board" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/interview-valve-on-their-insane-portal-2-ideas/)).
- *A dissent.* Jonathan Blow argued focus testing a puzzle game risks that "you start to get rid of the puzzles" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/jonathan-blow-talks-portal-2-and-player-freedom/)).
- *No solver.* No source mentions automated checking; Portal 2D's recorded solutions and solver go beyond anything Portal 2 had.

**The Puzzle Maker and the mapping community.** Matthew Gallant on the editor: a closed, grid-aligned test chamber with one entrance and one exit, binary inputs and outputs, several inputs on one device meaning AND, and connections "always visible to the player" as dotted lines ([The Quixotic Engineer](https://gangles.ca/2012/05/13/perpetual-testing-initiative/)). Portal 2D shares this grammar but for one thing: **it has no AND**. Two buttons or receivers on one element close it when either is released (guideline 4), so an AND has to be built as doors in series, or as a receiver switching on a second emitter. The community's rules, from the ThinkingWithPortals forum and Tuts+:
- "The player should always have all of the information in front of them"; "Teach cause and effect, not just cause or effect" (Idolon, [guidelines](https://thinking.withportals.com/forum/topic/portal-2-level-design-guidelines-t4291)).
- Portal surfaces are the difficulty dial: too few black walls opens simpler routes; white walls only in the exact spots removes choice (Silverman, [Tuts+](https://code.tutsplus.com/portal-2-level-design-creating-puzzles-to-challenge-your-players--gamedev-4828t)).
- 2–4 elements per chamber, each reused at least twice; "Dilemmas are your friend", such as one button that raises a panel you need and also one that stops you; an unused stretch of portal wall is fine, but not "a singled out portable wall between black walls that looks like it HAS to be used" ([puzzle producing process](https://thinking.withportals.com/forum/topic/puzzle-producing-process-t5983)).
- Make the central concept the final move, then add steps backwards (Demon Arisen, [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snZAq2GMOMU)).
- Make the first draft impossible, then add one interesting fix; remove skips that use "timing and accuracy, not thought", but not every solution that isn't yours; add a row of floor so a player arriving without the cube can walk back (Silverman, [Tuts+](https://code.tutsplus.com/portal-2-level-design-from-initial-idea-to-finished-level--gamedev-5321t)).
- "Popular maps are almost always dead easy", because only players who finish can vote (Mike Daas, [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L9BD004V9bI)).

**Portal Stories: Mel (Prism Studios, 2015).** A 22-level mod that skipped training ("we assume that you already know how to fire your portal"); reviews called it the Dark Souls of Portal games, and two months later Prism shipped an easier Story Mode as the default, keeping the originals as Advanced ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portal_Stories:_Mel)). From its developer commentary ([part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zja9oCowbX4)): even "obvious" rules need training; "we don't want people to be punished for trying things out", so a button opens a shortcut back to the cube source once an area is solved; a button used only once was cut; designers forgot how to solve their own puzzles after long breaks.

**Portal Reloaded (2021) and Bridge Constructor Portal (2017).** Reloaded's single developer refused to pad ("do something new with every puzzle"); its reviewers' main complaint was fragile state: bumping the present cube rewrote the future one and forced a full redo ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/portal-reloaded-is-the-closest-were-likely-to-get-to-portal-3/)). Bridge Constructor Portal kept portals fixed in the level, and reviewers missed placing them; its tiresome moments came "when you've solved the puzzle mentally but need to fine-tune the physical solution" ([PC Gamer review](https://www.pcgamer.com/bridge-constructor-portal-review/)).

**Borrow for Portal 2D.**
- Ray run on Portal 2's model: portal the ray (teach) → mirror box turns it in, then the box is also wanted on a button (test: when to take it out of the ray) → two rays, one mirror box, one portal pair (each solves one) → cut the ray on purpose (twist).
- "Cut it on purpose" works in Portal 2D because a receiver going dark switches its links back on: a lit receiver holding a light barrier off can be darkened by a box in the ray, switching the barrier on to stop a slide or block a shot.
- Fizzle to respawn: when the box is needed back at its start, destroying it is the move.
- Pull the rug: a box resting on a bridged ditch is destroyed when the link releases and returns to its start.
- One sightline past a fizzler: a single gap or a single wall that takes portals beyond it is the whole puzzle.
- A progress latch: once the first half is solved, a switch opens the way back to the box's start, so a lost box costs little.

**Doesn't carry over.** Flings, faith plates, gels, funnels carrying objects, turrets, timed buttons, analogue laser aiming, laser relays, AND-wired inputs.

### The Talos Principle (Croteam, 2014), Road to Gehenna (2015) and The Talos Principle 2 (2023)

**What it is.** First-person puzzle rooms (120+ in the first game) built from emitters, receivers, carried connectors that relay beams by line of sight (≈ Portal 2D's mirror box, portal pair, or a receiver that switches on another emitter), jammers that switch off doors and force fields while aimed at them, cubes, pressure plates, force fields, fans and a recorder. Optional "stars" hide behind unusual solutions.

**How the levels were made.**
- *Start from the catch.* "In the beginning, we used Lego elements to form labyrinths and gameplay elements, focusing only on the 'catch' of the puzzle"; most of those early puzzles survived into the final game (Davor Hunski, [GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/the-talos-principle-interview-ps4s-very-own-first-person-philosophical-puzzler-that-is-not-portal)). Puzzles were greyboxed from preset walls, and a script later gave each room its world's look, so the world could be chosen after the puzzle worked ([Ladavac, Nordic Game 2016](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D0ErxdacTT8)).
- *A hard puzzle creates the easy ones it needs.* Working on one interaction "forces you to create … several simpler versions"; when testers said a complex puzzle "would have been better if I knew before that I can use two jammers", the team made a puzzle that teaches exactly that first (Nordic Game 2016).
- *Obvious paths rarely work.* "Puzzles are designed in a way that solution is rarely straightforward; obvious paths rarely work" (lead designer Davor Tomičić, GamingBolt).
- *Rate, time and sort.* About 20 team members played all ~200 greyboxed puzzles, rated each for difficulty and fun and timed them; the team sorted them easy to hard, keeping duller teaching puzzles "so that people learn new concepts" with fun ones between (Nordic Game 2016).
- *Stuck is personal.* Some testers stalled on a puzzle everyone else found simple, then solved everything after it, so the worlds became hubs of puzzles open in any order (Nordic Game 2016).
- *A completion bot.* The game recorded each solution "in concept" (use this switch, put that item there) and a bot replayed it by pathfinding, so it survived geometry edits: a full run in about 20 minutes against about 10 hours for an expert, after every build. It caught a retextured door that no longer opened. "It cannot prove that there are no bugs … what it can prove [is] that it's possible to complete the game" (Nordic Game 2016; [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=162597)).
- *Stars.* Scattered stars delighted explorers and angered achievers, so every star became hard to earn, never found by accident (Nordic Game 2016).
- *Too much.* Andrew Plotkin found the curve smooth but about 90 puzzle areas "more than necessary", and "a time limit on thinky-puzzles just sucks" ([Zarf Updates](https://blog.zarfhome.com/2014/12/the-talos-principle-design-ruminations)).

**What Talos 2 changed.**
- *Overproduce and measure.* "There is certainly the double number of puzzles than there will be in the game"; cut puzzles were too easy, too hard, or "recycling the same idea"; many testers graded difficulty and fun for "actual metrics" where Talos 1's choice had been "subjective". A whole cluster was cut because opinion split "half and half and you cannot have half of players not liking your thing" ([Thinky Games, Puzzle Pruning](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KbMIp3q2Tc0)).
- *Sawtooth, not ramp.* In Talos 1 the last third was all complex puzzles, "mentally taxing … discouraging for some players", so Talos 2 spread the spikes: "not like a one continuous curve ramp … we have like many smaller ramps"; each area's new tool is taught in easy puzzles and combined with the others from about the fourth ([Thinky Games interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B3yoVxhld4Q); [Easy Allies](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e1TJ-vEB8-0)).
- *Designing out dead ends instead of undo.* A rewind prototype dropped players "somewhere in the middle of steps" with their thought lost, so it was rejected; instead "we thought about every puzzle where you could get stuck", and once known, most puzzles take "30 seconds or probably a minute" to redo (Thinky Games interview). That is the bar for a game with restart but no undo.
- *Timing removed.* The recorder: "some people liked it but most hated it", because "you have to be on the right spot at the right time" (same).
- *Exploits patched, or a fair route added.* Patch 1.1.0 fixed "easy exploits where the player could enter a puzzle in an unintended way and/or finish the puzzle without solving it"; in one puzzle it added a way to "turn off the fan instead of jumping into it" ([patch notes via mp1st](https://mp1st.com/news/the-talos-principle-2-update-2-005-out-for-version-1-1-0-this-jan-26)).
- *Mechanics as chess pieces.* Hunski: combined with other patterns of movement a mechanic "becomes like wow"; but "you cannot predict … how much potential does that have" (Easy Allies).

**Borrow for Portal 2D** (Talos tricks rebuilt on Portal 2D's rules, not copied: a ray stops at a closed door, and a ray-held door that is cut closes and blocks the ray itself, so Talos's "aim through the closed door" fails as copied).
- *A portal keeps what a door gave briefly.* Door D is held open by button B; while it's open, the only line to wall W runs through it. Put the box on B, shoot a portal onto W through the doorway, take the box to another button, let D close: the portal on W stays. A portal in the ray now sends it out beyond the door into the receiver. (Mind that the wall right behind a door takes no portal.)
- *Two jobs at the bend.* One mirror box must turn the ray onto the receiver and hold a button. Rays pass buttons, so a button on the corner tile lets the box do both; set up the wrong assumption by making another corner look like the obvious one.
- *Bootstrap chain.* Mirror box M1 waits behind door D1, which receiver R1 holds open; fetch M1 and use it to send the ray to R2, which opens D2 where M2 waits. The rules supply the catch: moving the ray off R1 closes D1, and a door closing on a box sends it home, so "carry it clear before you move the light".
- *Light goes where you can't.* An open ditch or a laser that is on keeps the subject out but lets rays and shots through.
- *Filters.* Portal 2D already has a full set of asymmetric barriers:

| # | Barrier (blocked or on) | Subject | Carried box | Portal shot | Ray |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Open ditch | Stopped | Stopped with the subject; a box lying there when it opens is destroyed | Passes | Passes |
| 2 | Laser | Stopped | Stopped with the subject | Passes | Passes |
| 3 | Fizzler | Passes, losing both portals | Destroyed | Stopped | Passes |
| 4 | Light barrier | Stopped | Stopped with the subject | Stopped | Stopped |
| 5 | Closed door or exit | Stopped | Stopped with the subject | Stopped | Stopped |

  A chamber where the subject, the box, the shot and the ray each reach a different region is a puzzle with no new element: ferry each across its own barrier.
- *Relay into a sealed room.* A receiver that switches on a second emitter relays the light past a wall, but cutting any segment darkens the whole chain.

### The Witness (Thekla, 2016) and Jonathan Blow's method

**What it is.** A first-person island of 650+ line-drawing panels on grids, eleven regions each exploring one rule family, taught only by the panels. Power cables show what powers what (≈ antlines). Its 3D environmental puzzles don't carry over; its sequencing and Blow's principles do.

**How the puzzles were made.**
- *Discover rules by asking low-level questions.* Panels use grooves because input devices vary; the line ends in a notch so you can't slip past; it may not cross itself because "you can't tell how many times I've circled that block". That last answer means every line splits the grid in two, and the partition rule followed: "it came just by asking these low-level questions; it didn't come from a top-down imposition" ([Blow and ten Bosch, IndieCade 2011](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OGSeLSmOALU)).
- *Virtues.* "Aim toward the richest space … explore it completely … trace a strong boundary … present [results] cleanly … with the lightest contrivance". Completeness means a matrix: a property such as "immune to rewind" has to be asked of every kind of thing in the world. Then surprise edits it: "take out all the things that are not surprising, except possibly for things that you need to tutorialize" (IndieCade 2011).
- *Truth, not hardness.* "You don't make good puzzles by trying to make things that are hard"; "look for what's true about the situation". An aha that doesn't let the player glimpse something true "is going to feel false and empty" (IndieCade 2011).
- *Clutter.* In Braid, levels got better "the more of that stuff that I eliminated". The Witness had buttons and levers "until I decided that was stupid. Because the players are looking for buttons and levers everywhere"; rooms stayed bare and only panels act ([noclip documentary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YdSdvIRkkDY)).
- *A sequence is a conversation.* The first partition run: a tiny panel ("only four solutions to this and two are right", so brute force is fine); the same stones with the exit moved, so winding the old way walls off the exit; two groups plus an empty square; the empty square moved, so the winding must reverse ("do you remember that? It was three puzzles ago"); then a stumper where both windings fail until you see the empty square mustn't be cut through: "you have to break your perception of that pattern" (IndieCade 2011).
- *Show both ends.* Right after the easy intro comes a puzzle "so hard that you actually just can't do it … we're obviously not asking you to do it", then simpler puzzles carrying the information: "it's okay if you can't do something" (noclip).
- *Difficulty is "a statistical thing".* "Different people find different things hard … I couldn't really rank the puzzles on a difficulty scale" (noclip).
- *Don't cut everything some find hard.* "If you design such that you kill anything that a percentage of people find hard … you end up getting this nucleus in the middle" (noclip). Croteam cut a cluster that split testers half and half; Blow keeps what a quarter of players hate. "When I playtest a game, I am almost never seeking feedback; it's more like executing a reality check" ([NYU Game Center](https://gamecenter.nyu.edu/?p=9486)).
- *Overproduce.* For his newer grid game, Order of the Sinking Star, Blow says the shipped puzzles are "probably less than half of what we designed and tried out" ([stream clip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ku64fkXIKzk)).
- *Attention by subtraction.* "We don't have to shine spotlights on things, because they don't stand out unless we mean for them to stand out"; cool props were vetoed because "people are going to walk up to it" ([Game Developer 2014](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-the-witness-i-modeling-epiphany); noclip).

**Borrow for Portal 2D.**
- *Same chamber, one thing moved.* Two chambers with the same walls; in the second, move the receiver to where the old route crosses the ray, or the exit behind the box's drop tile, so the first answer fails at exactly the step that shows the hidden rule.
- *A run of four that breaks its own pattern* (rays): one mirror box turns the ray onto a receiver; then the corner tile is a button; then two receivers; then a chamber that looks like the third but needs the portal pair instead of the mirror box, because the box is needed on a button out of line.
- *Completeness grid for each new element.* For rays: one row per element, asking what a ray does there, what happens when the subject cuts it there, and what happens when the element switches under a ray. Each surprising cell is a candidate chamber.
- *One kind of interactive thing.* Anything that looks like it could be an element should be one.

### Chip's Challenge (Chuck Sommerville, 1989), Chip's Challenge 2 (2015) and their level-pack community

**What it is.** A top-down tile game on 32×32 grids seen through a 9×9 window, in real time with monsters and a clock. Mechanically it is the closest grid game: ice (≈ slide floors, though its ice corners turn slides and pushed blocks slide too), force floors (they push; Portal 2D's arrows only restrict), toggle walls and buttons (≈ doors and links), blocks pushed onto buttons and into water to make a permanent bridge (≈ boxes, buttons, ditches), and teleports (≈ portals, loosely). Its community has produced five voted level packs (CCLP1–5) and the most explicit writing on what makes a fair grid level. Community jargon: a **bust** is an unintended solution, a **cook** a softlock.

**How the levels were made.**
- *Editorial control.* Sommerville designed about a third of CC1, a puzzle designer friend a third, and programmers from other Lynx titles the rest; "I was given the editorial control of which levels went into the game, and in what order". His idea generators: "think of ways to use an element in a manner that you have never seen it used before", and model a real process. Testers proved every level solvable and rated fun and difficulty, which set the order. He warned against levels "designed to be tedious, doing the same thing over and over again", preferring "lots of trade off decisions" ([Midnight Synergy, 2006](https://midnightsynergy.com/newsletter/issue004/page4.htm)).
- *Write the goals first.* J.B. Lewis (CCLP1's coordinator and the community's most-cited essayist) tells how a level planned as #39 grew mechanism by mechanism into #133; his fix: "Come up with a list of goals for your level… and check what you're doing with those original goals as you design" ([The Simple Things](https://forum.bitbusters.club/thread-91.html)).
- *One hard axis per level.* "What CC1 accomplished quite brilliantly was limiting its difficulty to one component… and dialing down other components": a long level keeps each part self-contained and forgiving; a level with one brutal puzzle "typically goes no further" ([In Defense of Consistency](https://forum.bitbusters.club/thread-81.html)). His four frustration amplifiers: convoluted logic, needless red herrings (worst late in a long level), excessive length, and unforgiving linearity where several options are shown but only one order works. One is fine; two or more make a level "relentlessly difficult" ([In Defense of Difficult Levels](https://forum.bitbusters.club/thread-83.html)).
- *Designer disconnect.* The designer sees the whole map and knows the answer; "Everything feels so obvious to us because we're the ones in charge". Designers also "felt obligated to fill up the entire map" (In Defense of Consistency).
- *Busts.* "Minor" busts skip one part, "major" ones change the objective ([wiki: Busted level](https://wiki.bitbusters.club/Bust)). The shapes that map onto Portal 2D:
  - An ice strip around the outside that bypasses a whole section, the biggest time-saving bust in any CC set.
  - A one-way tile next to a sliding tile.
  - Spare blocks or leftover items that make a section unnecessary.
  - The exit reachable by teleport.
  - A block carried through a teleport that ends the hazard guarding the exit.
  - A wrong link.
  - Rule differences between versions.
  
  One bust was found 34 years after release, mostly by optimisers chasing faster routes. In Portal 2D, the solver's fewest moves and fewest portals play that optimiser role.
- *Teaching.* CC1 front-loads eight lessons, each a small set of tiles with a one-line hint, then level 9 combines all eight, a jump first-time players found big; CC2 spreads its lessons out (levels 1, 9, 19, 29, 50, 60, 71). CCLP1 cut thieves from a lesson "to create a more steady difficulty curve", and moved a fake wall out of a no-return section because it felt "a touch unfair for a beginner player" ([wiki: Present Company](https://wiki.bitbusters.club/Present_Company), [Facades](https://wiki.bitbusters.club/Facades)). The tiny level *Tiny* teaches by an instant failure: the tempting move cooks the level at once, "but due to the small size this is an 'ohhhhh, so that's what I do' moment" ([CCLP1 Retrospective](https://forum.bitbusters.club/thread-63.html)).
- *The rule of 90/10.* In a good obscure puzzle, "the resources given to Chip will hint as to the solution", and the first steps hint at the rest ([wiki: CCLP2](https://wiki.bitbusters.club/CCLP2)). *Pier Seven* gives exactly seven blocks and one place where seven bridge the sea.
- *Don't be clever at the player's expense.* "If you think your red herring, guesswork challenge at the end, or making-the-player-realize-that-he-or-she-had-been-doing-everything-incorrectly-for-20-minutes moment is going to make someone go, 'Oh, that was just brilliant!', chances are that it probably will frustrate more people instead" (The Simple Things). Make the objective obvious, not the solution.
- *Hidden state was CC's main source of guesswork*: blue walls you can tell apart only by bumping them, ice mazes where you can't see a fall coming. Portal 2D shows every element's state, so it avoids this by construction.
- *Restart without undo.* Sommerville later called CC1 "pretty brutal", since one mistake 20 minutes in meant starting over, and made his later game's levels shorter ([Pocket Gamer, 2011](https://www.pocketgamer.com/chucks-challenge/interview-nifflers-chuck-sommerville-talks-about-how-chucks-challenge-on-iphone)). Lewis: full view plus undo lets a designer make far harder puzzles; CC "has no such mechanics" (In Defense of Difficult Levels). Portal 2D has the full view but not the undo.
- *Pack pipelines* ([wiki: CCLP3](https://wiki.bitbusters.club/CCLP3), [CCLP5](https://wiki.bitbusters.club/CCLP5)): dedicated playtesters, "fixing periods" for busts before voting, mandatory solution files, fun and difficulty voted separately with difficulty setting the order, easy levels "perfectly placed between the harder ones". "When evaluating easy levels, don't look for the interesting - look for the uninteresting" ([In Defense of Easy Levels](https://forum.bitbusters.club/thread-89.html)).

**Patterns that translate** (more in Part 4): a nine-square lattice of stopping points joined by ice lanes (*I Slide*); an ice field whose only landing points are the goals (*Ice Cube*); a block lodged on ice as a stopper; teleport "switch stations" where you change lines (*Blink*); ferrying objects across a network of islands (*Oversea Delivery*); exactly enough resources and one right place (*Pier Seven*); one key that must be used last (*Teleblock*); plug a teleport's exit so a slide stops elsewhere (*Partial Post*); show the goal blocked to state the concept at once (*The Sound of Silence*).

**Doesn't carry over.** Monsters and clone machines, time limits, random force floors ("levels that rely on random elements" are CC1's "least popular of all"), boosting and override timing, fake, invisible and hidden walls, one-use tiles, and the 9×9 window's thrill of discovery: in Portal 2D discovery has to come from understanding, not uncovering.

### Adventures of Lolo / Eggerland (HAL Laboratory, 1985–2000)

Single-screen 11×11 rooms, always fully visible, where "the most difficult thing about Lolo is figuring out the exact order that you should be doing things in" ([Retro XP](https://retroxp.beehiiv.com/p/retro-spotlight-adventures-of-lolo)). No designer interview exists; this rests on analysts and walkthroughs. Its overlaps: pushable framers (≈ boxes), Medusa statues whose gaze runs along their lines and is stopped by a block (≈ a ray cut by a box, with the stakes inverted: in Lolo you block a line to cross it, in Portal 2D you keep a ray clear to keep a receiver lit), trees that block walking but not the gaze against boulders that block both (≈ lasers and ditches against light barriers and closed doors), one-way arrows that don't stop shots, and an arrow power that flips one arrow (≈ a turnable arrow).

- *One element per opening room.* Eggerland Mystery's first rounds each add one monster; its Medusa room offers exactly the framers needed, one per gaze line ([StrategyWiki](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Eggerland_Mystery/Rounds_1-10)).
- *Curated for a new audience.* The US Lolo is a compilation of the easier, more straightforward rooms of its Japanese predecessors ([Hardcore Gaming 101](https://hardcoregaming101.net/eggerland/eggerland2.htm)).
- *Where it was unfair.* Eggerland 2's solutions were "nigh-impossible to figure out without a FAQ", needing requirements that "normally don't apply to the rest of the game"; a hidden respawn rule was "essential to solving some of the more difficult puzzles, which can be confusing". A later version animated the water currents because that "eliminates the need to figure this out by trial-and-error" ([Hardcore Gaming 101](https://hardcoregaming101.net/eggerland/eggerland.htm)). A reviewer's verdict on the rest: "It's never unfair: it just requires that you understand how the rules of its world work" (Retro XP).
- *HAL's later principle* (BoxBoy's director, who places it in Lolo's lineage): each world starts with a simple stage teaching a method, then a variation, then a combination; the stage that drew the most player questions required a technique never asked for before ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-guy-behind-one-of-the-year-s-best-3ds-games-1702235041)).

**Borrow.** *Prepare, then trigger*: Lolo's sleeping monsters wake on the last heart, so the room is solved by arranging framers before taking it. In Portal 2D: a final switch whose links open the exit and send the boxes home, so every button they held comes up and its door closes or its laser comes back on; the player must have placed portals so the route still works once the boxes are gone. The antlines show the trap in advance, which keeps it fair.

### The push-block family: Sokoban, Stephen's Sausage Roll, the Draknek games, Snakebird, Patrick's Parabox

Three differences from Portal 2D colour everything this family says. **Boxes are pushed there, carried here**: the classic deadlocks (a crate in a corner) can't happen in Portal 2D, and most box moves can be undone by picking the box up again; the difficulty moves into the cost of carrying (no portals with full hands), where a box goes down (a box is also a wall) and whether the box can be reached at all. **Every one of these games has undo; Portal 2D has only Restart**, so their warnings about dead ends weigh more here. **Portals join far-apart places**; the closest analogue in the family is Parabox's "walk into a box to enter it".

**Sokoban (Hiroyuki Imabayashi, 1981).** Imabayashi made stages "by trial and error. As a result of that trial and error, many stages remain unadopted"; he drew them on graph paper and tested them on friends ([interview](https://sokoboxes.com/articles/my-conversation-with-mr-hiroyuki-imabayashi/)). Levels 11–20 of the original had walls that broke when pushed from one side: "Some people got angry that it wasn't a puzzle," and he never used them again. Jonathan Blow calls them "really unfair stuff" ([clip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=As4HtC7q58I)). A US port shipped with an unsolvable level 42. David W. Skinner's *Microban* (2000), the community's model of small teaching levels, sorts its levels into four kinds: small levels showing one or two concepts; easier ones; "smaller and simpler versions of other levels"; and large levels with a single object ([Microban page](https://au-voleur.itch.io/microban-1)).

**What makes Sokoban hard for people (research).** Jarušek and Pelánek had 294 players solve 35 four-box levels of similar size; median times ranged from about a minute to almost an hour. State-space size didn't predict solving time at all (ρ ≈ −0.07), solution length only weakly (0.47); what predicted it was how often the solution switches which box it moves (0.74), and above all whether the boxes split into two independent groups (0.82). Two near-identical levels took a median of 3 minutes (splittable) and 54 minutes (not). Players spent only 14.5% of their time in dead states though 74% of states were dead, because they noticed and restarted quickly ([paper](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/stairs2010-final.pdf)). Taylor and Parberry, generating levels backwards from the solved state, reject open floor of 4×3 or more ("very bushy, but not very deep" searches) and tiles walled on three sides (dead space or a free parking spot), and count "box lines" rather than pushes, since long hallways are "not difficult, just tedious" ([paper](https://ianparberry.com/pubs/GAMEON-NA_METH_03.pdf)). Stuart Burfield (*Puzzledorf*) removed every decoy space: once a friend saw every element mattered, he "started to trust the puzzle" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/reflections-on-puzzle-design-in-puzzledorf)).

**Stephen's Sausage Roll (Stephen Lavelle, 2016).** A Sokoban-like that, as Alan Hazelden put it to Lavelle, "never really introduces new objects. It's just teaching you about new consequences of things that were already there"; Lavelle says that wasn't planned from the start ([Draknek & Friends podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4P2iC7YQu1k)). He sat on level ideas for months until a level turned up for them, settled unclear rule cases by "the direction that has more slapstick comedy value", and checked the game with one button that plays through every level. GMTK's reading of its level *The Clover*: each piece can be rolled straight onto its nearest goal, which leaves the player unable to reach the exit; the obvious assumption walks the player "right into the puzzle's central catch" ([GMTK, "What Makes a Good Puzzle?"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg)). Its gating (every puzzle in a section before the next) produced the notorious *Great Tower* wall ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/all-roads/)).

**The Draknek games (Alan Hazelden and collaborators: Sokobond, A Good Snowman Is Hard to Build, Cosmic Express, A Monster's Expedition; Bonfire Peaks by Corey Martin).**
- *Finding levels.* "The starting point is choosing a single interaction, or chain of interactions" that the solution must contain; elements go around it quickly, then a long iteration from minutes to years ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/the-relaxing-open-world-puzzle-design-of-i-a-monster-s-expedition-i-)). The Sokobond post-mortem describes the other way too: lay out a pleasing board, play it, ask "what did I just do, how can I stop you doing that", swing "between easy and impossible" until an interesting trick appears, then rebuild the level to force it ([post-mortem](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2mrqujss0Sk)). Martin: if you can't say what a puzzle is about, "it's probably not very focused" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/bonfire-peaks-explores-complex-emotions-through-puzzle-design)).
- *What a good level is.* "A good puzzle is almost always as small as it can be, with no unnecessary walking" ([Hazelden comment](https://www.electrondance.com/?p=12655)). Pieces "just spread out on the board" with no reason for where they are aren't engaging ([YagmanX interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RR9-5FyKJOM)). No filler: each puzzle "should feel distinct from what's come before"; he "absolutely hate[s]" runs of "ten repetitive easy puzzles" ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/developers-wont-hold-hand/)). The trick must be the only way through, because "if there's anything at all you can do even if it's unhelpful that doesn't involve using this trick people will do it" (Sokobond post-mortem).
- *Dead ends.* A level where one move makes it impossible "and it's totally nonobvious" teaches nothing: players "reset until they solve it" (Sokobond post-mortem).
- *Unintended solutions.* "Essentially it's a game of whack a mole"; sometimes an unintended solution is "interesting enough in its own right to be made into its own puzzle" (Monster's Expedition interview).
- *Teaching.* "You need to teach controls with tutorials, but you are generally better off teaching mechanics with in-game examples." Before a trick taught long ago is needed again, add a puzzle that brings it "to the forefront of your mind" (YagmanX).
- *Ordering.* Levels unlock next to solved ones, so the player has "not just one brick wall to bang their head against but three or four"; the hardest levels are optional; Sokobond cut about half its levels, Monster's Expedition kept one or two of a dozen versions. A required finale (Cosmic Express's *Nova 7*) is the most resented level (Electron Dance).
- *Tooling.* Monster's Expedition's editor runs inside the game, toggled by one key; every island is a JSON file in git with its intended solution stored as a replay; bugs become replay test cases; levels are tagged by mechanic and "intro"; edit-mode "X" markers show where land must never go "because it would cause unintended solutions" ([ThinkyCon 2024 talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98tmqN0bUGc)).
- *Bonfire Peaks* is the one game here whose boxes are carried, like Portal 2D's. Martin weighed what is physically intuitive against "what leads to interesting puzzles", and nearly gave up on the system as shallow before its depth showed.

**Snakebird (Noumenon Games, 2015).** Its growing body helps and hurts at once, like Portal 2D's carried box. GMTK's reading of level 10: "you're either too short or too long", so the obvious order fails (GMTK). A critic flags level 11, whose trick is only "eat the cherry on the first loop", as "an unrewarding puzzle that teaches little" ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/?p=13654)). Researchers built a Snakebird editor whose solver shows, per cell, how the shortest solution would change if an object went there; levels made with it had solutions about 50% longer, yet 28 of 34 designers preferred working without the overlay, and they misjudged which levels players would find harder ([Guzdial, Sturtevant and Yang 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.04147)).

**Patrick's Parabox (Patrick Traynor, 2022).** 364 puzzles shipped, "over 600 unused draft levels" ([GDC 2024 talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HAvS-RwkjdA)).
- *Simplify.* "Make the easiest possible version of a puzzle, that still features the interesting idea or point", trimming the possibility space so there are fewer things to do that aren't the solution ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/patrick-s-parabox-)).
- *Ordering is its own skill.* His tools: insert, modify, delete, reorder, make optional. Testers stalled at C after A and B, so he inserted a bridging puzzle; one still stalled, so C became optional. A seven-puzzle introduction that "just didn't work" lost five puzzles and gained a clearer first one and two reinforcement puzzles (GDC 2024).
- *Stumbling is fine if what follows checks it.* He's "okay with people totally stumbling upon a solution", "so long as they properly grappled with that same concept in the immediately following puzzles".
- *Testing.* Screen recordings with the tester thinking aloud; fresh, non-expert testers each time; batches of one or two, watching every video before changing anything; about 15 full-game tests, and afterwards he wished he had done more.
- *Ideas.* His list of 29 [strategies for puzzle level ideas](https://cwpat.me/misc/puzzle-level-idea-strategies/): force an interaction (his most-used); enumerate every pair of mechanics; enumerate action sequences; extreme geometry; make an impossible level possible and vice versa; gadgets (one-way tunnels, locks); "things almost happen" moments; turn alternate solutions into separate levels; translate puzzles from other games (Portal is his example); bisect two levels to fill a gap. Plus "show levels": trivial levels that just show a consequence, which players find delightful.
- *Rules.* A more flexible entry rule felt arbitrary and was one more thing to hold in mind, so he went back to the simple one; mechanics that combine badly went to a post-game appendix.

**PuzzleScript (Lavelle, 2013)** is the family's prototyping tool: grid rules as find-and-replace statements and levels as plain text. Monster's Expedition's first PuzzleScript levels survive almost unchanged in the final game. Lavelle doubts that player level editors pay off for puzzle games, since good levels are a small share of what gets made, which bears on Portal 2D's planned creator platform.

**Lessons for Portal 2D.**
- Make difficulty from tangled subgoals and moves against the obvious direction (one box needed on two buttons in turn, a portal placement for the first goal that breaks the second, carrying the box *away* from its button first), not from size or length.
- Every dead state should be either impossible or obvious the moment it happens; a reversible trap (a misplaced box can be picked up) is the fair kind.
- The Clover translates: in a one-tile corridor `S b . B . . E` (button B opens exit E), carrying the box to B from the start side and putting it down seals the corridor; the answer is to carry it over B, turn on the far side and put it down facing back. Reversible, and it teaches that a box bars its tile.
- Big open rooms give many choices but little depth; a one-tile nook is a free place to park a box and shoot. Have either only on purpose.

**Doesn't carry over.** Pushing, corner deadlocks, "baby steps", gravity and stacking, recursion, open overworlds, and undo-cushioned long chains where one wrong move ruins the run.

### Baba Is You (Arvi Teikari, 2019)

**What it is.** A turn-based, single-screen block pusher whose rules ("WALL IS STOP", "FLAG IS WIN") are themselves pushable words. About 220 levels, one designer, two years of testers. Its rule editing doesn't carry over, but its turn-based grid, whole level on screen and single designer make its process the closest to Portal 2D's situation.

**How the levels were made.**
- *Reverse engineering.* Teikari combined elements to find what surprised him ("what would happen if I have this and this thing coexisting?"), then built a level that forces that interaction so "the player has to see the cool thing to solve the level" ([GDC Plays](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RCk0bipJx84); [Game Developer 2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-i-baba-is-you-i-s-delightfully-innovative-rule-writing-system)). GMTK's breakdown of *Baba Doesn't Respond*: the solution is simple, and Teikari then added one restriction per shortcut, working backwards, "locking up doors behind him", which the player opens in the opposite order ([GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7zLwa4bztWs)).
- *Restrictions must lead to the catch.* Adding elements to hide an obvious answer "was sometimes also bad", because it could "drive the player's attention away from the cool twist" (GDC Plays). GMTK: the restrictions are "never red herrings or pointless busy work".
- *First levels fall out of the exhaustive pass.* Going through every meaningful interaction, "eventually I get levels where the 'trick' is mostly just the basic functionality of a specific element"; those open each area (GMTK).
- *Same room, roles swapped.* Level 2 is level 1's room with every role swapped, teaching that objects have no fixed meaning (GMTK).
- *Difficulty from what's missing.* Level 7 drops the WALL IS STOP rule almost every earlier level had, and players take a long time to notice they can walk through walls; he sees more potential in "difficulty [that] comes from what is missing instead of what is there" ([Gamelab 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8eTFea_UZcc)).
- *Overload.* "The levels very easily become overwhelming as the number of initially-active rules increases" (Game Developer 2019).
- *Wrong lessons.* Belts moving you inside walls felt intuitive to him, not to testers, so he removed them; "Almost every single level in the game was changed at least once" ([Talk & Dev](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v7ICYHL5MD4)). "DOOR IS SHUT" made players think SHUT meant solid: a naming failure of his teaching (GDC Plays).
- *Unintended solutions.* Allowed early, because "even the unintended, more trivial solutions often contain some element that's new to the player", then progressively restricted ([Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-hempuli-oy-s-i-baba-is-you-i-)). Some tester solutions became variant levels of their own ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/when-we-made-baba-is-you/)). One level kept an unintended solution he never fixed, "so most people don't solve it the way I thought" (Gamelab).
- *Engine edge cases.* Where rule combinations worked badly, he designed levels that never show them, "kind towards the players" ([GDC 2020](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jf5O8S5GiOo)).
- *Ordering.* Areas are grouped by the words they introduce, so each "resets the difficulty curve at least a little bit"; the map always offers several open levels, because "it's very easy to get mentally stuck"; only about 30 levels are needed to reach the end. He underestimated the difficulty: "very easy for that designer to become blind to a lot of the difficulty" (MCV).
- *Aim.* A surprised laugh: "it makes sense but I didn't expect it" (Talk & Dev).

**Borrow.**
- *Restriction layering.* Build the room where the intended move works with nothing else, then play it as an adversary; for each shortcut, add exactly one restriction and note what it blocks. Portal 2D's restriction kit: blocked walls, moving a wall that takes portals out of a sightline, a fizzler across the approach, a light barrier (blocks walking, shots and rays), a laser (walking only), shrinking the floor where a box can rest. Delete any restriction that blocks nothing: it has become a red herring.
- *The missing rule.* After several chambers where lasers and light barriers both appear as barriers, a chamber whose only wall that takes portals lies across a laser row: shots fly over lasers, where a light barrier would stop them. Or a door with an antline to a button and no box, so the subject holds the button itself and then reaches the door by portal.
- *Sacrifice as transport* (from GMTK's reading of *Tiny Pond*). A destroyed box goes back to its start, and a start can be a button: carrying the box into a fizzler from far away presses its start button again. Your guide lists this as a bypass to avoid (guideline 6); used on purpose, it's a twist.

### Lara Croft GO (2015) and Hitman GO (2014) (Square Enix Montréal)

**What they are.** Turn-based puzzles on a board of nodes and lines, the whole puzzle on screen, no text tutorial. Levers (≈ switches), pressure plates (≈ buttons), pushable pillars that fill gaps, block boulders and kill enemies (≈ a box that touches many systems), trapdoor pairs (≈ portals), and KO_OP's "eyes" in its expansion that fire when something passes in front and are blocked by pillars (≈ emitter, ray and receiver, cut by a box).

**How the levels were made.**
- *Bare first.* "We created all the nodes and the lines first, and only later did we dress them up" ([Game Developer 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/for-square-enix-designing-i-hitman-i-as-a-board-game-just-made-sense)). Tooling in-engine, one right-click per block: "if tools are super easy to use then the designers are just going to be excited to use them" ([BIG Festival 2016](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e94xwaizTS8)). They made "way, way, way more levels" than shipped.
- *Cut the universal verb.* Hitman GO dropped a "wait" action because "it basically allowed you to kill anyone on the board" (Game Developer 2014).
- *Unwritten rules.* When a plate pushing a spider onto a boulder raised "what happens?", they decided "to just design our puzzle so this never happens", building up "all these unwritten rules about how to create levels". No solver, "designers and QA"; in the expansion "someone found a way to break the puzzle" ([GDC 2016 postmortem](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r3A-DY9iDnU)).
- *Tedium.* Maze-like exploration levels were cut: "too much swiping required with too little decisions to make" ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/?p=65919)).
- *No words.* "A game with no words, no tutorial, so we just have people try"; if a tester stumbled, back to the drawing board (BIG 2016). The lever-door countdown went through about 100 visual treatments: teaching is partly a presentation problem.
- *Difficulty.* The level designers' first puzzles were "insanely hard", and were cut; the post-launch chapter raised difficulty sharply but came after the story, reusing the levels too hard to ship (GDC 2016). Hitman GO's optional goals (briefcase, no kills, move limits) earn stars.
- *Visible progress.* A hub door with three locks, each key fetched from a maze, so players always see progress towards a goal.

**Borrow.** Keep a written list of states chambers must never reach (Portal 2D has "States to avoid", plus a solver Lara GO lacked). Compare the solver's fewest moves with the number of "walk then act" steps in the route: a high ratio flags walking chores. Let the exit and a chain of doors with lit antlines show progress.

### Q.U.B.E. (2011) and Q.U.B.E. 2 (2018) (Toxic Games)

First-person rooms where the player's gloves work only on coloured blocks, white surfaces staying inert: the same contract as Portal 2D's walls that take portals and walls that don't. Q.U.B.E. 2's designer built each puzzle in an empty square room before the art team dressed it: "The prettiest room in the world would just frustrate player if they couldn't find the exit" ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/the-production-of-q-u-b-e-2)). The first game used most mechanics once and felt unfocused, so the sequel limited cube types; each mechanic is shown working in a fixed position before the player can place it, and story stays out of puzzle rooms because "people can't focus on two things at once". Testers weren't screened for puzzle experience, so the game was made easier and fans called it too easy: "The important thing is you need to get the right players" ([Game Developer 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-the-i-qube-2-i-devs-built-a-better-massive-3d-puzzle-labyrinth)). Readability: "The player should be able to easily identify every puzzle element in the room."

**Borrow.** Show it in place before handing it over: a first ray chamber can start with a mirror box already resting in the ray, turning it into a wall, one tile from where it would aim at the receiver. Count each element's uses across the campaign; a mechanic used once is cost without payoff.

### Quantum Conundrum (Airtight Games, Kim Swift, 2012)

Single rooms in a mansion; the player switches the whole room between dimensions using batteries placed per room. Mechanics were chosen for "the most permutations of use" ([Game Developer 2011](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/kim-swift-on-creating-i-quantum-conundrum-i-)) and settled at four "without overwhelming our players". Reverse gravity threatened to break the climbing puzzles, so rooms withhold it by withholding the battery: "being able to pace out the dimensions in each puzzle was a big help" ([CES interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iQOIYQJUA2o)). Players who saw an object they couldn't reach would "thrash on it for a really long time", so such bits were removed. A room should be read fast: "within 30 seconds to a minute, know what they have to do"; watch for "accidentally giving negative reinforcement for the right thing to do"; "leading the player without making them feel like they're led" ([Game Developer 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-inclusive-design-of-kim-swift)).

**Borrow.** Portals are Portal 2D's overpowered tool: decide per chamber which wall sides may take them and remove the rest. No unreachable bait: a box, button or switch visible in a sealed area reads as a target. The one-minute test: can a first-time player say *what* they must achieve within a minute? The chamber sign's pictograms and the antlines help.

### Antichamber (Alexander Bruce, 2013)

A first-person game of impossible spaces with no death and a map to jump back anywhere. In 2009 "I basically had the whole game built but it was totally not understandable"; he then picked the most introductory puzzle for each concept and put it first ([design3 interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WpNd6OqA4Hg)). Testers said "I'm always lost. There's too many dead ends"; lessons taught in one area were needed in another, so he connected spaces so each lesson sat next to its use. At E3 people played five minutes and left; after fixes the problems showed up "20 minutes through rather than 5 minutes through": how far players get before stalling is his measure ([GDC 2014](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wOlcB-JxkFw)).

**Borrow.** Put the lesson next to its use: the chamber after a teach chamber should use its lesson at once. Design wrong moves to reveal information rather than trap.

Each topic gives the points the sources agree on, each sourced, then what it means for Portal 2D. The tag at the end of a point compares it with `docs/level_design.md`: **[covered: G#]** means your guide already says it (guideline number), **[new]** means it doesn't, **[tension]** means sources disagree or the point pulls against the guide.

### 3.1 Core design workflows: how to build a chamber from scratch

**"Forwards" and "backwards" mean four different things.** Read any advice with that in mind:

| # | Workflow | What it means | Who | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Exploratory forwards | Play with the rules until something surprising turns up; package it as a puzzle. You don't know the answer when you start | Blow and ten Bosch ([IndieCade 2011](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OGSeLSmOALU)), VanDevander ([talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0zVjdEhHmGo)), Brett Taylor ("the mechanics came up with the levels", [GDC 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B36_OL1ZXVM)) | Recommended for *finding* ideas |
| 2 | Backwards from the trick | Pick the one interaction the solution must contain, build the smallest room where it is the only way, then add one restriction per shortcut | Hazelden, Martin, Traynor, Teikari, Brown, Croteam (see Part 2) | Recommended for *building* a chamber; the most common method among the designers researched |
| 3 | Backwards from the goal | Start at the exit and ask what each step needs, as a dependency chart; aim for a diamond shape (one solve opens two or three, which reconverge) | Ron Gilbert ([dependency charts](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)), Noah Falstein ([report](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-technique-lucasarts-used-to-design-its-classic-adventure-games)), Demon Arisen (central concept as the final move) | Recommended for multi-step chambers |
| 4 | Backwards as obfuscation | Take a solved state and shuffle it until it's hard | VanDevander names it to warn against it: difficulty becomes arbitrary | Avoid |

**The method most of the designers converge on**, in order:
1. **Write the one moment the chamber is about as a sentence** before placing a tile. Brett Taylor calls it the "solution sentence", "the secret to a magic trick": if the player knew it, the level would be trivial ([GDC 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B36_OL1ZXVM)). Corey Martin: if you can't say what a puzzle is about, "it's probably not very focused" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/bonfire-peaks-explores-complex-emotions-through-puzzle-design)). Croteam started from "only the 'catch'" ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/the-talos-principle-interview-ps4s-very-own-first-person-philosophical-puzzler-that-is-not-portal)); Valve from one teaching goal per chamber ([Game Informer 2010](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber.aspx)). **[new]**
2. **Build the smallest room where that moment is the only way through.** "A good puzzle is almost always as small as it can be, with no unnecessary walking" (Hazelden, [comment](https://www.electrondance.com/?p=12655)); Traynor makes "the easiest possible version of a puzzle, that still features the interesting idea" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/patrick-s-parabox-)). **[covered in part: G36 "no larger than its puzzle needs"]**
3. **Play it as an adversary; for each shortcut add exactly one restriction, and write down what it blocks.** Teikari works backwards "locking up doors behind him" ([GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7zLwa4bztWs)); Silverman makes the draft impossible, then adds one interesting fix ([Tuts+](https://code.tutsplus.com/portal-2-level-design-from-initial-idea-to-finished-level--gamedev-5321t)). Delete any restriction that blocks nothing: it has become a red herring. **[new]**
4. **Run the noise test.** "Describe the solution of the puzzle and remove anything you didn't mention" (Taylor, who vetted every level at least four times). The one good reason to keep an unmentioned piece is to stop an accidental solve. **[new]**
5. **Find the variations.** Traynor: one puzzle often became four keepers (easier, harder, an extra constraint) ([GDC 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HAvS-RwkjdA)). The easier variants are your teach chambers; this is how "a hard puzzle creates the easy ones it needs" (Croteam's two-jammers story, [Nordic Game 2016](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D0ErxdacTT8)). **[covered in part: G42]**

**Where ideas come from on demand.**
- *The pair matrix.* Put every element on both axes of a table and treat each cell as a prompt: two elements in an empty room, play until something surprising happens, then build backwards (Brown, [Developing 9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=akeVPZLZejY); Taylor; Traynor). Portal 2D already has this table: the interaction table in `docs/elements.md`. Each cell names the rules that bring two elements together; the surprising ones are chamber candidates. **[new]**
- *Completeness, then surprise.* For each element, ask each property question of every other element ("what if this is on a slide floor? in a ray? behind a fizzler?"), then cut the cells a player would predict, keeping those a tutorial needs (Blow, IndieCade 2011). **[new]**
- *Testers' near-misses and unintended solutions.* "A sign of a promising puzzle game is when I can play test three levels and come away with ideas for 10" (Hazelden, [YagmanX](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RR9-5FyKJOM)); turn an unintended solution into its own chamber (Hazelden, Teikari, Traynor). **[new]**
- *Traynor's list of 29 strategies* ([cwpat.me](https://cwpat.me/misc/puzzle-level-idea-strategies/)), the most useful for a small grid: enumerate action sequences (shoot then carry, carry–put down–shoot–pick up); extreme geometry (every wall takes portals, or one does); "possible, then impossible, then undo the last change" to find the tightest version; gadgets (arrows as one-way valves, a switch as a latch); translate puzzles from other games (Portal is his own example).
- *Sit on it.* Lavelle carried some level ideas for months before a level turned up for them ([podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4P2iC7YQu1k)); Blow found The Witness's rules only after coming back months later "with an open mind" ([Truth in Game Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C5FUtrmO7gI)).

**Expect waste.** Parabox shipped 364 of about 960 drafts; Sokobond cut about half; Talos 2 made "the double number of puzzles"; Blow kept "less than half"; A Monster's Expedition kept one or two of a dozen versions (sources in Part 2). For the 10 chambers still to make, budget about 20–30 drafts. **[new]**

**Keep a card per chamber**: its sentence, the elements it uses, its role (teach, test, twist, combine) and what it needs the player to know already. Brown's "puzzle bible" made gaps visible ("level 4 suddenly adds four new elements") and turned each gap into a brief ([Developing 9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=akeVPZLZejY)); Hazelden tags every level by mechanic and "intro" so a pool of drafts can be searched later ([ThinkyCon 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98tmqN0bUGc)). Your 04b chamber plan table is most of this already. **[new]**

**Tentpole first.** Valve's Matt Wood builds "my ultimate challenge for the player" first, finds the "three or four things in here which the players don't really understand", and designs the chambers before it to plant them ([Kiwi Talkz](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3dYAZxr2FbY)). Teikari chose Baba's final level in the first two months ([Gamelab 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8eTFea_UZcc)). For a short run of four, design the combine chamber first and derive teach, test and twist from it. **[new]**

### 3.2 Mistakes to avoid

**Tedium.**
- *Walking is not thinking.* Lara Croft GO cut its maze levels: "too much swiping required with too little decisions to make" ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/?p=65919)). Taylor added walking to stop brute force, and it didn't make anyone think. Taylor and Parberry count "box lines", not pushes, because long hallways are "not difficult, just tedious" ([paper](https://ianparberry.com/pubs/GAMEON-NA_METH_03.pdf)). **[covered in part: G36]**
- *Execution after the insight.* Portal 1's quitters left after the two hardest-to-execute puzzles ([Wolpaw](https://www.engadget.com/2011-03-13-valves-erik-wolpaw-on-portal-2s-difficulty-ps3-steam-features.html)); Talos 2's bar is a 30–60 second redo once you know the steps ([Thinky Games](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B3yoVxhld4Q)). A turn-based grid gets most of this for free; don't bring it back through long move sequences after the idea has clicked. **[new]**
- *Filler.* Hazelden "absolutely hate[s]" runs of "ten repetitive easy puzzles"; Taylor would cut his six-level series to three ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/developers-wont-hold-hand/); GDC 2019). **[new]**

**Unfair dead ends** (Portal 2D has no undo, so these weigh more here than in any game researched).
- *Hidden dead ends teach nothing.* A move that makes the level impossible "and it's totally nonobvious" just makes players "reset until they solve it" ([Sokobond post-mortem](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2mrqujss0Sk)). Players leave dead states fast *once they notice*: in Sokoban research 74% of states were dead but players spent only 14.5% of their time in them ([Jarušek and Pelánek](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/stairs2010-final.pdf)). So the cost of a dead end is the time before the player notices it, plus the restart. **[covered in part: G15 "a chamber that can be lost on purpose should make it obvious"]**
- *Three ways out, in order of preference*: design them out (Talos 2: "we thought about every puzzle where you could get stuck"); make them visibly fatal at once (Wilderplace's designer, via [Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/?p=16930)); make the reset cheap (Grant on a Hearthstone puzzle whose slow reset made experimenting "very tedious", [30 lessons, part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)). Portal 2D's Restart is instant, which helps. **[covered: G15–G18, "States to avoid"]**
- *Recovery built in.* Valve added box dispensers so players are "always ... supplied with the required tools" ([commentary, TC13](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)); Portal Stories: Mel opens a shortcut back to the cube once an area is solved ("we don't want people to be punished for trying things out", [commentary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zja9oCowbX4)). Portal 2D has two built-in recoveries: a destroyed box returns to its start, and a switch can send a box home. A switch reachable from where the box might get stranded is a rescue path. **[covered in part: G5, G10]**
- *Fear of failure kills experimenting.* Long death animations made Portal 2 co-op players "afraid to take risks" ([commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)); Juul found the punishment players resent is setback, being made to replay ([Fear of Failing?](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). Keep chambers short enough that a restart isn't a punishment. **[new]**

**Clutter and noise.**
- Every element must matter. Clutter "interfered with the portal training process" (Valve, [commentary, TC05](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)); Braid got better "the more of that stuff that I eliminated" (Blow); The Witness removed buttons and levers "because the players are looking for buttons and levers everywhere" ([noclip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YdSdvIRkkDY)); Puzzledorf's player "started to trust the puzzle" once he saw every cell had a purpose ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/reflections-on-puzzle-design-in-puzzledorf)). **[new]**
- *Clutter creates shortcuts, not difficulty.* Refraction's researchers found distractor pieces "often only serve to give players the tools to build a simpler solution" ([Smith, Butler and Popović 2013](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/smith2013qop.pdf)). Before adding an element to hide the answer, ask what simpler solution it enables. **[new]**
- *Unreachable bait.* Players who saw an object they couldn't reach would "thrash on it for a really long time" (Kim Swift, [CES interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iQOIYQJUA2o)); a box, button or switch in a sealed area reads as a target. **[new]**
- *Overload.* Teikari: levels "very easily become overwhelming as the number of initially-active rules increases" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-i-baba-is-you-i-s-delightfully-innovative-rule-writing-system)); Puzzledorf's testers quit a dense level before trying it: "It was not the difficulty that scared people off, but having too much information to take in at a glance." The Portal 2 community settles on 2–4 elements per chamber, each reused at least twice ([ThinkingWithPortals](https://thinking.withportals.com/forum/topic/puzzle-producing-process-t5983)). **[new]**

**Unintended shortcuts.** "So much of puzzle design is about forcing the player to do one very specific thing" (Brown, Developing 9). Even veteran Refraction designers shipped levels with undesirable solutions (Smith et al.). Valve's policy: rework a chamber when players "bypass chunks of the puzzle too easily", keep a "ninja" skip that takes more skill than the intended route (commentary, TC14). **[covered: G38, "The puzzle is bypassed"]** Portal 2D-specific holes the research turned up:
- *Shooting through an open door.* Shots pass an open door, so "stand on the button, shoot through the doorway, skip the box" (Portal's TC04 cheat) is open in every box-and-door chamber unless the line is broken. Valve blocked it with glass; Portal: The Flash Version made it the answer of Task 20. Decide per chamber which it is. **[new]** (guideline 14 lists what stops shots, but not this pattern)
- *A walking route that works will be taken.* In a turn-based game there's no time cost, so a slower route never steers anyone: Valve's slow-lift fix in Portal's TC15 doesn't translate. Close the route. **[new]**

**Mode confusion.** "Players assume inherently that there's no agility needed in puzzle games"; break that once and they suspect reflexes everywhere (Taylor, GDC 2019). Portal 2D is strictly turn-based, but slides and walk-outs animate: nothing should ever look like a timing check. **[new]**

**Wrong lessons.** Teikari's "DOOR IS SHUT" made players think SHUT meant solid (GDC Plays); Valve's weighted button meant for the player sent testers "searching hopelessly for a box" (commentary, TC11). Watch for "accidentally giving negative reinforcement for the right thing to do" (Swift, [Game Developer 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-inclusive-design-of-kim-swift)). **[new]**

### 3.3 Introducing mechanics without text

- **First contact is forced, safe and unambiguous.** Miyamoto's mushroom bounces off the first pipe back into the player ([Iwata Asks](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-New-Super-Mario-Bros-Wii/Volume-1/4-Letting-Everyone-Know-It-Was-A-Good-Mushroom/4-Letting-Everyone-Know-It-Was-A-Good-Mushroom-210863.html)); Puzzledorf's first tutorial is a single forced path ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/tutorial-design-in-puzzledorf-reflections)); Mega Man X traps the player in a pit where the only thing to do is the new move, with a reward just outside ([Sequelitis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8FpigqfcvlM)). **[covered: G40 teach]**
- **Gate understanding, so stumbling fails.** Valve rebuilt TC01 so "stumbling around will virtually always lead to a dead end" (commentary, TC01). **[tension]** with Traynor, who is "okay with people totally stumbling upon a solution", "so long as they properly grappled with that same concept in the immediately following puzzles" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/patrick-s-parabox-)), and Blow, whose first panel has four paths, two right. Both agree on the condition: a stumble is fine only if the next chamber checks the idea.
- **One solution early, several later.** "For training purposes, there's generally just one correct solution to these early puzzles"; later puzzles open up (Faliszek, [commentary, TC04](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)). In Portal 2D: a run's teach and test chambers allow one route, its twist and combine chambers may allow several. **[new]** (guideline 40 asks only that a teach chamber's mechanic be the only way forward), but **[tension]**: Teikari let unintended solutions stand early, because "even the unintended, more trivial solutions often contain some element that's new to the player", and restricted them later ([Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-hempuli-oy-s-i-baba-is-you-i-)).
- **Show it before requiring it.** Demonstrate a consequence on something unimportant first: "Letting players see other characters make mistakes that they'll need to avoid is an effective way to explain your puzzles" (Birdwell, [The Cabal](https://www.jmeiners.com/shamans/papers/art/half_life_design.pdf)); Q.U.B.E. 2 shows each mechanic working in a fixed position before the player can place it ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-the-i-qube-2-i-devs-built-a-better-massive-3d-puzzle-labyrinth)). In Portal 2D: a box the player doesn't need, lying where a door closes or a fizzler switches on, shows destruction and return before the player's own box is at stake; a mirror box already resting in a ray shows the turn. **[new]**
- **Make the player stop where it matters.** Testers rushed past what made the portals, so TC02 got "a mandatory pause in the action - what we call a gate" (Brashill, [commentary, TC02](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)). Turns rush no one, but a held key repeats and a slide carries the subject past, so where a chamber shows something the player must notice, make the route stop in front of it (a door to open, a button to press, a slide that ends facing it) rather than run past it. **[new]**
- **Correct the likely wrong belief.** Testers assumed orange was exit-only, so TC03 forces entering orange (commentary, TC03). Chamber 5 does this already for "the other end can be moved". **[covered in part: G42]**
- **Consistent cues build associations, and associations stick.** Portal's fling cue was learned "in much the same way that players learn to associate cubes with buttons" (commentary, TC10). Once early chambers always pair a button with a box, a button meant for the subject alone breaks the habit: good for a twist, confusing in a teach. **[new]**
- **Take the training wheels off at the end of a run.** Portal's last fling room removes the cue: "players must realize that they can double fling almost anywhere" (commentary, TC15). **[new]**
- **Re-teach after a big new mechanic.** Testers forgot flinging after the full gun arrived, so TC12 brings it back (commentary, TC12); Hazelden refreshes an old trick just before it's needed again ([YagmanX](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RR9-5FyKJOM)). **[new]**
- **Fix the sightline, not the text.** Portal 2's Fizzler Intro tested badly until a single gap at eye level, valid from either side, made the shot obvious ([commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)). Make the rule visible when it fires: shots now flash on Portal 2's fizzlers. Portal 2D already does this with the fizzler splash, the light barrier reaction and the miss ripple. **[covered: game feature]**
- **Show the wiring.** "Don't put a button on the ground and make the player stand on it to see what it does" (Grant, [30 lessons](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)); Portal 2 draws every connection, and the Puzzle Maker's dotted lines are automatic ([Gallant](https://gangles.ca/2012/05/13/perpetual-testing-initiative/)). **[covered: G33]**, but **[tension]**: G33 lets later chambers drop antlines, which turns "which button does what" into part of the puzzle. The Portal 2 community calls hidden key information "artificial difficulty" ([Idolon](https://thinking.withportals.com/forum/topic/portal-2-level-design-guidelines-t4291)). Drop an antline only where finding the link is meant to be the puzzle.
- **Don't drag.** Juul's testers of a casual game: "After 20 minutes, felt like I saw the whole game" ([Fear of Failing?](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). Mel skipped training and had to ship an easier mode ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portal_Stories:_Mel)). Both failures exist: one teach chamber per element, then move on.
- **Show levels.** Trivial levels that just show a consequence, which Parabox players found "delightful": "You don't always need a challenge" (Traynor, GDC 2024). **[new]**
- **Put the lesson next to its use.** Antichamber's fix for "I'm always lost" was to connect each teaching puzzle to where it's applied ([design3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WpNd6OqA4Hg)). **[covered: G43]**

### 3.4 Difficulty and pacing

**What makes a puzzle hard** (the research is consistent):
- *Not size, not length.* For 35 near-identical Sokoban levels, state-space size didn't predict solving time (ρ ≈ −0.07) and solution length only weakly (0.47). What did: the number of moves that go *against* the obvious direction (0.69), how often the solution switches which box it moves (0.74), and above all whether the problem splits into independent parts (0.82). Two near-identical levels took a median of 3 minutes (splittable) and 54 (not) ([Jarušek and Pelánek 2010](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/stairs2010-final.pdf)). For puzzles whose moves can be undone, like Rush Hour, length predicted difficulty well (ρ ≈ 0.90) ([2011](https://cdn.aaai.org/ocs/2518/2518-11200-1-PB.pdf)); Portal 2D sits between the two.
- *Bottlenecks.* Players spend most of their time before one narrow passage of states and almost none after; "the bottlenecks are what create difficulty", and two in one level multiply the time (Grant, [part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)). One key move per chamber is plenty.
- *Presentation alone.* The same Tower of Hanoi structure presented differently varied up to 16:1 in difficulty (Kotovsky, Hayes and Simon 1985, via secondary sources); Brown shows a near-identical puzzle easy in The Turing Test and hard in Portal 2 because of how it's laid out ([GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg)). Start positions are a dial: Portal 2's Triple Laser went from "permanently stuck" to fine with "incredibly subtle changes in the cubes' initial positions" ([Foster](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/p/level-with-me-adam-foster-part-2.html)).
- *For laser-and-mirror puzzles specifically*, a study fitted player ratings of Lazors from features such as reflections in the solution and beam crossings; the counts of emitters and receivers barely mattered ([van Kreveld et al. 2015](https://ics-websites.science.uu.nl/docs/vakken/mscip/assignments/CIG2015-AutomatedPuzzleDifficultyEstimation.pdf)).
- *Designer heuristics* (Square Enix Montréal, via [GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg)): the number of possible solutions, the number of steps, the number of options at each moment, and which mechanics must already be known. Too many options overwhelm; too few let players "just do the thing that's in front of them, over and over again, until they solve the puzzle" (Brown, Developing 9).

For Portal 2D: **make a chamber harder by tangling its subgoals, not by enlarging it** (one box needed on two buttons in turn; a portal placement for the first goal that breaks the second; a move that carries the box *away* from its button first). **[new]** The solver's fewest moves and fewest portals are only part of the picture; how often the route switches between box, portal pair and slide is a better proxy. **[new]**

**The designer can't judge it.** Teikari: "very easy for that designer to become blind to a lot of the difficulty" ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/when-we-made-baba-is-you/)); Brown: the creator "just cannot gauge their quality or difficulty"; Snakebird researchers found designers misjudged difficulty even with solver numbers in front of them ([Guzdial et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.04147)). **[covered: G39, G43]**

**Pacing shape: a sawtooth, not a ramp.** Linehan and colleagues coded Let's Plays of Portal, Portal 2, Braid and Lemmings: each skill is introduced alone in a simple puzzle, practised and combined, and complexity rises until the next skill resets it ([CHI PLAY 2014](https://cora.ucc.ie/handle/10468/3461)). Talos 1's steady ramp made its last third "mentally taxing … discouraging", so Talos 2 used "many smaller ramps" ([Thinky Games](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B3yoVxhld4Q)). Baba's areas each "reset the difficulty curve at least a little bit" ([GDC Plays](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RCk0bipJx84)). **[covered: G40, G41]**

**Rests.** Valve broke up hard chambers with simple ones and turned an elevator into a rest after "elevator fatigue" ([commentaries](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)); Taylor added big easy levels "to let people exhale". **[new]**

**The finale needn't be the hardest.** "The more complex, the longer players would take, resulting in bad pacing"; Portal's most climactic moment was "about the easiest puzzle in Portal" ([GDC 2008 slides](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2008/GDC2008_PortalPostMortem.pdf)); Cosmic Express's required finale is its most resented level ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/all-roads/)). Portal had time pressure for drama; Portal 2D doesn't, so its last chamber needs synthesis and spectacle, not the hardest catch. **[new]**

**Optional peaks and parallel options.** Almost every game researched keeps its hardest puzzles optional (Talos 2's lost puzzles and Golden Gates, Lara Croft GO's post-story chapter, Snowman's optional levels, Baba's extras) and offers several open puzzles at once, because "it's very easy to get mentally stuck" (Teikari). Portal 2D's chambers are a numbered list, but none is locked: a stuck player can already open the next. **[tension]**: Valve's co-op hub, which let players pick any chamber, "prevented us from doing sufficient training", so it went linear ("By going linear, we could guarantee prior knowledge", commentary). With 20 chambers and no optional layer, the closest equivalents are (a) the par block for fewest moves and portals, which Valve itself moved out of the story into optional Challenge maps because "we were never quite able to sell it" (commentary, TC13), and (b) a late chamber that is hard and nothing after it depends on.

**Ordering is a skill of its own.** Traynor's verbs: insert, modify, delete, reorder, make optional. When testers stalled at C after A and B, he inserted a bridging puzzle; when one still stalled, C became optional ([GDC 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HAvS-RwkjdA)). Foster: fix a too-hard chamber by changing the ones before it ([Radiator Blog](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/p/level-with-me-adam-foster-part-2.html)). Croteam had ~20 people play every greyboxed puzzle, rate difficulty and fun, time them and sort ([Nordic Game 2016](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D0ErxdacTT8)). **[covered in part: G43]**; the bridging-chamber move is **[new]**.

### 3.5 Creating "aha" moments instead of trial and error

- **Assumption → catch → revelation** (Mark Brown, [What Makes a Good Puzzle?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg), informed by Hazelden and Square Enix Montréal):
  - The goal must be obvious: "The player shouldn't be figuring out what to do - just how to do it."
  - The catch is "a logical contradiction, where two things are seemingly in direct conflict"; the minimal case is a button that opens a door you can't reach while standing on it.
  - It resolves by changing the order, starting from a different position, or (the "gold standard") a revelation: a "non-obvious - but also totally logical consequence of the game's rules" that joins the player's toolbox.
  - The best puzzles lure the player into a wrong assumption that walks them into the catch. That gives a starting point instead of overwhelm, builds a model of the puzzle while chasing the wrong idea, all but guarantees a first failure (so the player feels stumped instead of stumbling into the answer), and focuses them on the catch. Weak puzzles have a catch too easy to resolve, no assumption (so players stumble into it), or too little revelation (so it feels like busywork). **[new]**
- **Why it works (psychology).** Insight problems stall at an impasse until the solver drops a constraint they imposed on themselves; dropping a broad, deeply trained belief is harder than a narrow, local one ([Knoblich et al. 1999](https://www.kognition.uni-koeln.de/literature/Knoblich_etal_1999.pdf)). So a twist that breaks something earlier chambers trained hard is a harder aha than one that breaks a local assumption. Moves that seem to go away from the goal are among the strongest difficulty predictors (Jarušek and Pelánek). **[new]**
- **Truth, not hardness.** "You don't make good puzzles by trying to make things that are hard"; an aha that doesn't show something true about the rules "is going to feel false and empty" (Blow, [IndieCade 2011](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OGSeLSmOALU)). Grant separates eureka (the pleasure of understanding, which survives being shown the answer) from fiero (pride in beating a challenge) and warns against designing for fiero ([part 1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oCHciE9CYfA)). **[new]**
- **Trick versus revelation.** A Braid puzzle that works at only one moment "felt more like a trick than a revelation", partly because players couldn't experiment toward it (Brown). Snakebird's level 11, whose trick is only "eat the cherry on the first loop", "teaches little" ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/?p=13654)). No one-off tricks: a method used once leaves players paranoid on every later puzzle (Grant). **[new]**
- **Kill trial and error.** "If the solution can only be reached by trial and error or plain luck, it's a bad puzzle" ([Gilbert 1989](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)). Keep the steps few but the chance that a random attempt works low (Blow). Gate so that stumbling dead-ends (Valve TC01). Taylor's red flags in playtests: "was that right?", "oh, I solved it". Always-on rays invite wiggling: an accidental solve "can rob the player of understanding" ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/reflections-on-a-design/)), so put the insight where wiggling can't reach it, such as which wall must hold a portal or where a box must already be before a slide. **[new]**
- **Plant fragments; don't hand over the idea.** A puzzle right before Portal 2's *Bonk Heads*, "where two cubes repeatedly collide", cut solve time but "almost completely robbed the appeal"; fragments planted four maps earlier let players "own that exciting dual collision epiphany" (commentary). Wood: teach a thing and "you want them to forget about it just long enough" ([Kiwi Talkz](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3dYAZxr2FbY)). **[new]**
- **Same pieces, new consequence.** Stephen's Sausage Roll "never really introduces new objects. It's just teaching you about new consequences of things that were already there" (Hazelden to Lavelle, [podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4P2iC7YQu1k)). This is your twist chamber's job description. **[covered: G40 twist]**
- **Difficulty from what's missing.** Baba's level 7 drops a rule nearly every earlier level had; players take a long time to notice ([Gamelab](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8eTFea_UZcc)). **[new]**

The ones every source names: the catch (two goals in conflict), the lure (an easy earlier use primes a wrong move), both orders fail (interleave), the single key move, the counterintuitive move, the hidden consequence, prepare then trigger, a proxy for being in two places, the reprise (same layout, one change), the sequence that breaks its own pattern, and the alternate solution turned into its own level.

### 3.7 Testing and validation

**Playtesting.**
- *Watch in silence; trust behaviour.* Valve's Half-Life testers played for two hours while designers sat behind them, "not allowed to say anything"; a session gave about 100 action items ([Birdwell](https://www.jmeiners.com/shamans/papers/art/half_life_design.pdf)). "Game designs are hypotheses / Playtests are experiments"; think-aloud is useful but "inaccurate and biased", and "People don't know why they do what they do" ([Ambinder, GDC 2009](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/GDC2009_ValvesApproachToPlaytesting.pdf)). Read body language, not what testers say (Swift, [IGS 2007](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2F0SVZA9fIo)). **[covered in part: G39]**
- *A session script* (from Schell-based course slides, [PDF](https://stewart.sdsu.edu/cs583_spr2013/LearningXNA4_lects/JesseSchell_playtesting101.pdf)): go in with specific questions; tell testers you're testing the game, not them; say you won't help; "You should be writing down about one new problem per minute"; watch the face more than the screen; "Don't ask your playtesters to be game designers"; "Don't defend your game". Valve also ignores the fixes testers suggest ([GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9Yomqk0C6kE)). **[new]**
- *Record.* Traynor used screen recordings with testers thinking aloud, fresh non-expert testers each time, batches of one or two, and watched every video before changing anything; about 15 full-game tests, and afterwards he wished he'd done more ([GDC 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HAvS-RwkjdA)). Croteam asked testers "why did you do that when you could have done something else". **[new]**
- *How many.* Five testers per round find most usability problems; three rounds of five beat one round of fifteen, since fixes need retesting ([Nielsen](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/); usability research, but the iteration argument transfers). A person is a first-time player of a chamber only once ("tissue testers"), so ration them: stagger which friend sees which chamber first, and keep one or two untested for the final order ([Eastshade](https://eastshade.com/2015/10); Puzzledorf). **[new]**
- *Who.* Testers unscreened for puzzle experience made Q.U.B.E. too easy for its fans: "The important thing is you need to get the right players" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-the-i-qube-2-i-devs-built-a-better-massive-3d-puzzle-labyrinth)). Valve mixed "total Portal newbies" and "Portal pros" weekly ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/interview-valve-on-their-insane-portal-2-ideas/)).
- *Stance.* "It's not the player's fault -- it's your fault" (Swift, [GDC China 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/10-years-of-design-lessons-from-em-portal-em-s-kim-swift)). **[tension]** on what to do with negative feedback: Croteam cut a cluster that split testers half and half ([Thinky Games](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KbMIp3q2Tc0)); Blow warns that cutting "anything that a percentage of people find hard" leaves "this nucleus in the middle", and keeps what a quarter of players hate ([noclip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YdSdvIRkkDY)). With no optional chambers, Croteam's stance fits Portal 2D's required list better.
- *Retest after every change.* A retextured door that silently stopped opening broke Talos until a bot reached it ([Nordic Game 2016](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D0ErxdacTT8)); a texture changed behind a ladder broke a Left 4 Dead session ([Faliszek](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/valve-s-faliszek-playtesters-aren-t-idiots-it-s-you)). **[covered: G37, G38, replay check]**

**Machine checks.** No source shows Valve using a solver for Portal or Portal 2; validation was human. Portal 2D's recorded solutions, replay check and solver already go beyond them, and match the best practice found: Stephen's Sausage Roll had "one button that can just play through the whole game"; A Monster's Expedition stores the intended solution of every island as a replay and turns bugs into replay test cases ([ThinkyCon 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98tmqN0bUGc)). **[covered: G37, G38]** What the research adds:
- *Prove the negative.* A designer proves a puzzle *has* a good solution; a check must prove *no* solution is a shortcut, which takes exhaustive search ([Smith et al.](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/smith2013qop.pdf)). A tester beating the designer's best by two moves made one developer write a solver ([Wallerstein](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/just-a-level-solver-why-my-simple-puzzle-game-took-me-over-a-year-to-complete)). **[covered: G38]**
- *Concept-required check.* Tag each chamber with the moves it introduces, requires and wants players to try and fail; check that required moves appear in every solution ([Vet and van Rozen 2024](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/34332/34332.pdf)). G38's "remove the links to an element, expect unsolvable" is one case; the general form also covers moves ("solvable with zero portals?", "solvable without picking up the box?"). **[new]**
- *Near-miss census.* List the dead states within one or two actions of the intended route: those are the mistakes players will actually make. Each should be visibly fatal or deliberately instructive (synthesis of Vet and van Rozen's near-solution failure traces and Grant's "negative confirmers"). **[new]**
- *One-change sweeps.* One added object can swing a solution a lot: one spike in a Snakebird level took the shortest solution from 28 to 41 moves (search snippets of [Sturtevant et al. 2020](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/7421)); in Fling!, adding or removing one ball flipped a level between solvable and unsolvable ([Cook](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-saturday-paper-exhausting)). For a draft, script the solver over single changes (one wall face set to refuse portals, one wall tile added or removed, a box start moved one tile) and list which changes lengthen the route or remove an unintended shorter one. Keep the human as the judge. **[new]**
- *Forbidden facts.* Monster's Expedition's editor shows "X" markers where land must never go "because it would cause unintended solutions". A per-chamber list of "must never be possible" facts (this face never takes a portal; this tile is never reached before the box is placed) could be checked by the solver. **[new]**
- *Draw the graph.* For slides, every tile a slide can stop on is a node and every slide an edge; for links, the reachable state graph (Johansen's [PuzzleGraph](https://blog.runevision.com/2016/04/puzzlegraph-puzzle-state-space.html): "every change in state should be significant and correspond to a choice made by the player"). Both expose dead ends and false choices a layout sketch hides. **[covered in part: G20, G24]**

### 3.8 Player psychology

- **Players should blame themselves.** "The players should always blame themselves for failure": warn of the danger and show a way out, and they try harder; fail them without warning and they blame the game (Birdwell, [The Cabal](https://www.jmeiners.com/shamans/papers/art/half_life_design.pdf)). Players who blamed "the game was too hard" rated it lower ([Juul](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). **[new]**
- **Stress blocks learning.** "Players Learn Better When Not Stressed" ([GDC 2008 slides](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2008/GDC2008_PortalPostMortem.pdf)); frustration triggers fight-or-flight and inhibits problem solving, and stuck players fall into a "cognitive rut" that a break can fix ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2017/11/22/why-you-get-stuck-in-games-and-what-you-can-do-about-it)). In a turn-based game the stress is complexity: teach in a calm, small chamber before a combine chamber needs it.
- **Trust is a resource.** Decoys cost it (Puzzledorf), ambiguous solutions cost it (Taylor), one-off tricks and inconsistent rules cost it (Grant), and a secret found without its clue makes players search every corner from then on ("poisoning the well", Bob Bates via Grant). Players push through "this seems impossible" only for a designer they trust (Grant's "aporia"). **[new]**
- **Everything visible reads as meaningful.** "The perceivable part of an affordance is a signifier", and accidental cues are read like deliberate ones ([Norman](https://jnd.org/signifiers-not-affordances/)); a "weird awkward niche" is "probably there for a reason" (Sean Barrett, [Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/all-roads/)); Valve made decorative catwalks reachable because testers kept trying to reach them (commentary, Escape). Players also assume objects stay where they're useful: Foster's testers never moved a cube once it powered something. On a single-screen grid with no decoration, every tile shape is read as intent. **[new]**
- **Match what matters with what stands out.** "Things that are important should stick out. Things that stick out should be important" (Grant, [part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)). Eyes go to movement and light ([Unity](https://unity.com/blog/level-design-basics); Swift: players move "towards the light"). In Portal 2D, lasers, open portals and rays glow, fizzler fields drift and light barriers flicker, so an animated element is noticed before a static one: a moving distractor pulls harder than a still one. **[new]**
- **Fair and unfair red herrings.**
  - *Unfair:* decoration salient enough to seem pertinent, an affordance you never need, leftovers of removed puzzles (Grant's "dressings", "distractors" and "ghost puzzles"); a "rabbit trail", a wrong approach that can't be proven wrong, which is "the most demotivating, especially for novices" (Grant). "I never design with red herrings. The players will create their own" (Eric Harshbarger via [Room Escape Artist](https://roomescapeartist.com/2019/02/10/red-herrings/)).
  - *Fair:* a lure built from real rules that the player's own reasoning produces (Brown's assumption), or several plausible uses of the same real elements, only one of which works. An unused stretch of portal wall is fine; "a singled out portable wall between black walls that looks like it HAS to be used" isn't ([ThinkingWithPortals](https://thinking.withportals.com/forum/topic/puzzle-producing-process-t5983)).
  - *Test:* whether it regularly misleads testers, whatever the intent (Spira). **[new]**
- **Confirm progress.** "Realizing that you're on the right track is one of the most dopaminergic things" (Grant). In Portal 2D, an antline turning amber, a door opening or a receiver lighting is a confirmer; a first sure sub-goal within the first few moves hooks the player (Gilbert: "Letting the player know at least the first sub-goal is essential"). **[new]**
- **Separate independent sub-puzzles visibly.** Portal 2 co-op players who could wander between areas "logically assumed that the individual puzzles were part of one big puzzle" (commentary, airlocks). **[new]**
- **Hints.** Portal 2D has none, and the evidence doesn't push for them: in Refraction (50,000 students) every hint design tested lowered performance ([O'Rourke et al. 2014](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/orourke2014hsm.pdf); children in an educational game, so transfer is unproven). Talos's own hint system was "too hard to get any hints at all" (Ladavac). Clearer chambers and earlier fragments beat hints. **[new]**

## Part 4 — Pattern catalog, mapped to Portal 2D

Patterns from the games and the generic writing, grouped by the Portal 2D element they rest on. Each row names the source, says how it's built with Portal 2D's elements, and adds a check against your rules where one applies. Sources are linked in Parts 2 and 3; the mechanic-specific writing (ice puzzles, one-way tiles, laser mazes, switch logic) is summarised at the end of each section.

**Five facts about Portal 2D that change how outside patterns translate:**
1. **No AND gate.** Two buttons or receivers on one element close it when either is released or darkens (guideline 4). Portal 2's "several inputs on one door" becomes doors in series, or a receiver that switches on a second emitter whose ray reaches a second receiver.
2. **Links only open things when activated.** Pressing, using or lighting unblocks; releasing or darkening blocks again. "Press X to close Y" can't be built directly. Exclusive states have to come from layout: one box for two buttons, a mirror box steering a ray between two receivers, a turnable arrow, a switch that sends a box home (which releases its button).
3. **No hidden state, no timing, no momentum, no undo.** Every pattern that relies on fake walls, a clock, speed or an undo cushion is out. Slides are the only "keep moving" rule, and they resolve within one step.
4. **Carrying, not pushing.** Push-block deadlocks don't exist; a box is also a wall while it rests; full hands mean no portals.
5. **Shots pass open doors, lasers, ditches, boxes and arrows.** Most portal shortcuts come from this (guideline 14).

| 7 | **A portal keeps what a door gave briefly** | Talos connector puzzle, rebuilt | Box on button, shoot through the open door, take the box elsewhere, let the door close: the portal stays and can carry a ray or the subject beyond |
| 8 | **One sightline past a fizzler** | Portal 2's Fizzler Intro | A single gap in a fizzler run, or a single wall beyond it that takes portals, is the whole puzzle |
| 9 | **Switch stations** | Chip's *Blink* | Slide lanes joined by portals, with plain-floor tiles where the subject stops, turns and re-shoots |
| 10 | **Ferry across islands** | Chip's *Oversea Delivery* | Islands separated by open ditches; each hop with a box is put down, re-aim, pick up, cross (no shooting while carrying) |
| 11 | **Arrow as a firing post** | This document, from Portal 2D's rules | On an arrow the subject faces only its way, so the arrow fixes the shot's direction; a turnable arrow chooses which wall a tile can hit |

Writing on 2D portal games is thin: only small itch.io projects (Portal Crates in PuzzleScript, Top Down Portal), Mari0 and Gateways turned up, none with design notes beyond "let the player choose the exit" (Portal 2's Carousel, where Valve found it "more instructive", [commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)).

| 2 | **Solved but trapped (The Clover)** | Stephen's Sausage Roll | A one-tile corridor `S b . B . . E`: putting the box on B from the start side seals the corridor; carry it over B and put it down facing back. Reversible; teaches guideline 9 |

### 4.4 Slide floors

| # | Pattern | Source | In Portal 2D, and what to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Stopping-point graph** | Ice-sliding research (Dorbec et al., [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.00559)); Pokémon ice solvers | Every tile a slide can stop on is a node, every slide an edge. Check the goal is reachable only through the intended node and every node has an exit that leads somewhere new (guidelines 20, 24) |
| 2 | **Lone stopper teach room** | Ryan Krause's *Escape Exam* ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/let-s-talk-level-design)) | A small slide room with one wall tile in the middle. Portal 2D differs from Pokémon: a plain-floor tile stops the subject *on* it, a wall *before* it. Worth showing early |
| 3 | **The box as a placeable stopper** | Ricochet Robots helper robots ([Hůla et al.](https://people.ciirc.cvut.cz/~janotmik/ICAART-23.pdf)); Zelda ice blocks ([Kluiver thesis](https://theses.liacs.nl/pdf/2019-2020-KluiverS.pdf)); Chip's blocks on ice | Portal 2D inverts Zelda: the subject is the ice block and boxes are rocks it places. **Caution:** on an open field, three free ice blocks make every target reachable (Kluiver); a carryable box is cheaper still, so constrain where it can go down or give it a second job |
| 4 | **The overshoot** | Kluiver ("will slide past targets unless stopped") | A button mid-lane is pressed only by stopping on it (guideline 21); the slide version of "press the button" needs a stopper placed first |
| 5 | **Portal as a redirector** | Ricochet's diagonal walls (left out of first games by its rulebook) | A slide into a portal comes out heading the way the other portal faces: a player-placed diagonal wall. Introduce after plain slides (guidelines 25, 26) |
| 6 | **Half-bridge valve** | Andrea Gilbert's tilt mazes ("every significant decision you make is non-reversible", [clickmazes](https://clickmazes.com/newtilt/new-ixtilt2d.htm)) | A lane with a stopper at one end and none at the other is a one-way valve built without arrows. With no undo, each one is a real commitment |
| 7 | **Nine-square lattice** | Chip's *I Slide* | A 3×3 lattice of plain stop tiles joined by slide lanes, some walled off; portal walls at lane ends make the graph player-editable |
| 8 | **Landing points only at goals** | Chip's *Ice Cube* | Scattered plain tiles in a slide field, some holding buttons. A button on slide floor doesn't stop a slide; a button on plain floor does |
| 9 | **Backward from the goal, then fill** | Kluiver's generator | Start at the goal tile, ask what stops the subject there, walk back. Then let the solver find shortcuts; turning unused slide floor into plain floor is the direct fix |
| 10 | **Barrier as a stopper** | Lara Croft GO expansion's eyes; Portal 2's *Turret Blocker* | A light barrier switched on in a lane stops the slide in front of it. Close to your own slide-bridge idea in `level_design/levels.txt` |

From the mechanic-specific writing: Fire 'n Ice's design "shines most when it locks Dana down in a tight area" and weakens into hallways and mini-puzzles ([review](https://birthbydrip.neocities.org/reviews/fire_n_ice)); an ice maze that sends you back to the start on a wrong move tests only patience; a solver needed about 44 times more search on a layout with two fewer stones: each stopper removes more options than it seems to ([Sootopolis write-up](https://evoniuk.github.io/posts/sootopolis.html)). Doesn't carry over: sliding objects, cracking ice, spin tiles that push.

### 4.5 Arrows and turnable arrows

| # | Pattern | Source | In Portal 2D, and what to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The valve that ends backtracking** | Boris the Brave on lock-and-key dungeons ([blog](https://www.boristhebrave.com/2021/02/27/lock-and-key-dungeons)); Unexplored | An arrow in a one-tile corridor right after a section is solved, so the player never needs to come back |
| 2 | **The key lies beyond the valve** | A tabletop write-up of Mark Brown's Boss Keys graphs | Everything the route needs after an arrow must be past it or reachable back over it by portal (guideline 5) |
| 3 | **One-way loops force deliberate navigation** | Boss Keys on Link's Awakening ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AezAN2RcO8Y)) | An arrow ring travelled one way; shots fly over arrows, so a portal can cut across it: the intended shortcut or a hole |
| 4 | **Carrying the key through one-way space** | Boss Keys on Eagle's Tower's iron ball | On an arrow the subject faces only its way, so a box can go down only there; "get the box and the subject to the same place through one-way space" is a puzzle in itself (guidelines 8, 9, 18) |

Doesn't carry over: forced motion (Pokémon spin tiles, Chip's force floors push; Portal 2D's arrows only restrict), player-held rotation powers.

### 4.6 Rays, emitters, receivers and mirror boxes

| # | Pattern | Source | In Portal 2D, and what to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Portal the ray** | Portal 2's Laser Intro | Your chamber 8 |
| 2 | **Mirror box shown in place** | Q.U.B.E. 2 (show it working before handing it over) | The mirror box already rests in the ray, turning it into a wall one tile from where it would hit the receiver |
| 3 | **Mirror first, then needed elsewhere** | Portal 2's Laser Stairs; the community's "mirror first, portals later" | The mirror box turns the ray onto the receiver, then is needed on a button; the portal pair has to take over the ray |
| 4 | **Two rays, one mirror box, one portal pair** | Portal 2's Dual Lasers | Each tool solves one ray. In Portal 2D the receivers can't share a door (no AND): chain them, or give each its own door in series |
| 5 | **Cut the ray on purpose** | Portal 2's Laser Over Goo; Antichamber | A dark receiver switches its links back: a light barrier comes on (stops a slide, blocks a shot), a laser comes on (destroys a box lying in it, which flies home), a ditch opens (same). | 6 | **Toggle between two receivers** | Zelda's crystal switch (via Boss Keys), rebuilt | A mirror box on the ray's line sends it to receiver B; without it the ray goes on to A. Putting the box down or picking it up flips which door is open |
| 7 | **Relay chain** | Talos connectors; Laser Maze checkpoints | Receiver R1 switches on emitter E2, whose ray must reach R2. Cutting any segment darkens the chain, so route the first segment through a portal pair to keep it off the subject's path |
| 8 | **Two jobs at the bend** | Talos (one tripod needed twice) | A button on the ray's corner tile lets one mirror box both turn the ray and hold the button |
| 9 | **Bootstrap chain** | Talos *Ray Trivia* and similar | Each freed mirror box frees the next; moving the ray off R1 closes D1, and a door closing on a box sends it home: carry it clear before you move the light |
| 10 | **Light goes where you can't** | Talos *Through a Window*; Lolo's trees | An open ditch or a laser that is on keeps the subject out but lets rays and shots through |
| 11 | **One listening face** | Khet's one-sided pieces | A receiver facing away from the emitter's line forces the ray to arrive through a mirror box or a portal |
| 12 | **Many targets, few redirectors** | Portal 2's Triple Laser | Fewer mirror boxes than seem needed, plus a portal pair that substitutes for one; one box already sits in a ray and must be moved |

From the laser-and-mirror writing: always-on beams invite wiggling, and an accidental solution "can rob the player of understanding" (Joel Goodwin on Chromatron, [Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/reflections-on-a-design/)); "more things on the screen does not necessarily translate into more fun" (same); a laser-puzzle developer's testers couldn't tell which way emitters faced or tell mirror variants apart, and lost track of buttons under boxes ([Photon Phaser devlog](https://chloegregg.itch.io/photon-phaser/devlog/811484/main-feedback-spoilers-yet-again)): check `/` against `\`, the receiver's face and the emitter's face at about 40 px tiles. Doesn't carry over: splitters, colours, free angles, beams that hurt or push, laser relays that a beam passes through.

## Part 5 — Chamber ideas, by element and part

Prose sketches, not layouts: none has been built, solved or tested. Each names its part (teach, test, twist, combine, as in guideline 40), the pattern it comes from, and the guidelines to check when building it. They're grouped by element and slotted into neither plan.

What already exists, so the ideas avoid repeating it: chambers 4 and 5 teach and test slide floors, 6 and 7 teach and twist turnable arrows, 8 and 9 teach and test rays through portals, and the 04b file lists six ideas left by stage 04a (a fizzler mid-slide, a box carried across slides, an arrow held by a box, arrows changing where a slide ends, a ray crossing the tile before its own door, a mirror box carried around a fizzler). Gaps worth noting: **no chamber uses a light barrier or a mirror box yet**, and only chambers 1 and 3 use fizzlers.

### 5.1 Portals (basics)

1. **Gated islands** (teach or test). Three or four floor islands in a row, separated by lasers or open ditches, each with exactly one wall face that takes portals and sees the next island. The only way forward is a crossing per island, in order; a wrong shot is fixed by re-shooting. *From:* Portal TC01's gating. *Teaches:* aiming from the right tile, and that either portal can be the entrance. *Check:* guideline 17 (each island has a face it can shoot from to come back), guideline 12 (no island's face is the wall at the end of a laser run).
2. **Shoot past what you can't walk past** (laser teach). A corridor to the exit cut by a run of lasers; one wall beyond the run takes portals, one wall on the subject's side does too. *From:* Portal: The Flash Version's blue plasma wall. *Teaches:* lasers block walking, not shots. *Check:* the wall beyond must not be the end-plate wall of the laser run (guideline 12).
3. **Hold the door with a shot** (door teach, or a twist after box-and-button chambers). A button opens a door, and there is no box. Standing on the button, the subject faces the open doorway and a wall beyond it that takes portals; it shoots, steps off (the door closes), shoots the second portal on its own side and crosses. *From:* Flash Task 20, the cheat Portal's TC04 blocked. *Teaches:* open doors pass shots; a door closing doesn't close portals. *Check:* the far wall must not be the tile right behind the door (those faces never take portals); after this chamber, every box-and-door chamber must decide whether this move is allowed (Part 3.2).
4. **The missing rule** (light barrier teach by contrast, or laser twist). Two routes to the exit area: one cut by a light barrier run, one by a laser run, each with a wall beyond that takes portals. Shooting across the light barrier misses (the band reacts); across the laser it works. *From:* Baba's level 7 ("difficulty from what is missing"). *Teaches:* light barriers stop shots, lasers don't. *Check:* the light barrier's own end-plate walls take no portals (guideline 12); give the light barrier no link, so it stays on.

### 5.2 Boxes, buttons and doors

5. **The Clover corridor** (box twist). A one-tile corridor: start, box, floor, button, floor, floor, exit, where the button opens the exit. Carrying the box to the button and putting it down from the start side seals the corridor with the exit open beyond it; the answer is to carry the box over the button, turn on the far side (turn key) and put it down facing back. *From:* Stephen's Sausage Roll's *The Clover*. *Teaches:* a box bars its tile; the put-down tile is the one faced. *Check:* guideline 1 (button not next to the exit), guideline 9; it's fully reversible, which is why it suits a game with no undo.
6. **Hands full** (box test). The button sits in a room reached only by portal. Carrying the box, the subject can't shoot, so it must shoot first, or put the box down, shoot and pick it up again. Escalation: the only tile that sees the right wall is one the subject reaches while carrying. *From:* the carry rule plus Snakebird's "too short, too long". *Check:* guideline 18 (a tile to put the box down on wherever the subject can arrive carrying it).
7. **One box, two buttons in turn** (box combine). One box must hold button A (opens door A) and later button B (bridges a ditch or opens door B); taking it off A closes door A behind the subject, so the order and a portal placed while A was held decide the route. *From:* Jarušek and Pelánek's decomposition finding; Chip's *Teleblock*. *Teaches:* nothing new; it's a test of planning. *Check:* guideline 7 (box starts not on tiles where it could land under the subject), guideline 1.
8. **Counterintuitive first move** (box twist). The box starts beside its button, but putting it there first blocks the corridor to the only wall the subject needs to shoot. The answer carries it away, shoots, then comes back. *From:* the counterintuitive-move metric. *Check:* guideline 9 (a reachable tile beside the box wherever it rests).
9. **Prepare, then trigger** (switch combine). The exit opens only by a switch whose links also send both boxes home, so the buttons they held come up and their doors close; the player must have placed portals beforehand so the route to the exit survives. The antlines show what the switch will do. *From:* Lolo's sleeping monsters. *Check:* guideline 10 (switch, not button, sends boxes home), guideline 6 (box start tiles), guideline 7.

### 5.3 Fizzlers

10. **Set your portals first** (fizzler test). Box on one side of a fizzler that is on, button on the other. Walking the box through destroys it and closes both portals; the answer is to place both portals from a tile whose shot lines miss the field, then carry the box through the portals. *From:* Portal TC09. *Check:* guideline 13 (don't make the player stand in the field to aim), guideline 12 (the walls behind the connectors take no portals).
11. **Fizzle to respawn** (fizzler twist). The box starts on button B1, which holds door D1 open. The route needs the box elsewhere first (taking it closes D1), and later needs D1 open again from a side where B1 can't be reached (past an arrow). The answer: carry the box into the fizzler; it flies home onto B1 and D1 reopens. *From:* the Portal 2 community's "fizzle to respawn"; Baba's *Tiny Pond*. *Teaches:* a destroyed box goes to its start, wherever that is. *Check:* guideline 6 (this is its "box delivered by its start tile" used on purpose), guideline 7 (the subject is never on B1 when the box lands), an antline from B1 to D1 so the effect reads.
12. **Cheap first failure** (fizzler teach). The obvious walk carries the box straight into a fizzler that is on: it breaks up and lands back at its start two tiles away, and both portals close. Nothing is lost but a few steps, and the way round (a portal pair placed first) is then plain. *From:* Chip's *Tiny* (an instant, cheap failure in a tiny room), Birdwell ("see other characters make mistakes"). A box can't *start* on a fizzler, a door or a laser, so in Portal 2D the demonstration has to be one the player causes. *Check:* guideline 5 (the start tile stays in reach), guideline 13.

### 5.4 Light barriers

13. **A wall you can switch** (light barrier teach). A room split by a light barrier run held off by a button and its box; the barrier blocks walking and shots, so neither the subject nor a shot crosses until the box is on the button. *From:* first contact forced and safe. *Check:* guideline 1 (button not next to the band), guideline 2.
14. **Barrier as a stopper** (slide and light barrier combine). A slide lane overshoots the tile beside the exit; a light barrier across the lane, held off by a receiver lit by a ray, comes on when the player puts a box in the ray, and the next slide stops in front of the band. *From:* the Lara Croft GO expansion's eyes; Portal 2's *Turret Blocker*. Close to your own slide-bridge idea in `level_design/levels.txt`. *Check:* guideline 30 (the ray must not cross the barrier's tile), guideline 20 (work out every stop with the band on and off), guideline 31.

### 5.5 Ditches and blocked walls

15. **Ferry across islands** (portal and box combine). Islands separated by open ditches, crossed only by portals; the box must reach the last island. Each hop is put down, re-aim, pick up, cross. *From:* Chip's *Oversea Delivery*. *Check:* guideline 18 (a tile to put the box down on each island), guideline 17 (a way back from each island while it matters).
16. **Pull the rug** (ditch twist). A box rests on a bridged ditch, held by a button; releasing the button destroys the box, which flies home to a start tile beyond a barrier the subject can't carry it past. *From:* Portal 2's *Pull The Rug*. *Check:* guideline 1 (never under the subject), guideline 6 (the start tile is meant to be reached this way).
17. **Steel that opens** (blocked wall teach). The only wall that sees across the gap is a blocked wall, which takes portals only while a button holds it; the box goes on the button, the portal goes on the wall, the subject crosses. *From:* Portal's staged tool; Quantum Conundrum's per-room battery. *Teaches:* blocked walls refuse portals until a link opens them. *Check:* guideline 2 (no side of the linked blocked wall reachable by crossing from the button), guideline 13 (its portal vanishes if the wall is blocked again).
18. **The portal budget** (blocked wall combine). One switch unblocks a group of walls, changing which faces take portals across the whole chamber; the player must choose when to use it, since switches can't be undone. *From:* Ocarina's Water Temple via Boss Keys. *Check:* guideline 4 (only the switch links those walls).

### 5.6 Slide floors (beyond chambers 4 and 5 and 04a's ideas)

19. **Box as a stopper** (slide twist). A long lane ends at a dead end; the exit (or a side door) is one tile before the end. Without the box the subject overshoots; with the box on the end tile it stops beside the door. Escalation: the only box is also needed on a button, so a portal has to take over one of its jobs. *From:* Chip's blocks on ice; Ricochet's helper robots. *Check:* guideline 20, guideline 24, and Kluiver's caution that free boxes make most of an open field reachable.
20. **Nine-square lattice** (slide and portal combine). A 3×3 lattice of plain stop tiles joined by slide lanes, some walled off; walls at lane ends take portals, so the player edits the graph. *From:* Chip's *I Slide*; switch stations from *Blink*. *Check:* guideline 24 (every stop tile has a move that leads somewhere new), guideline 25 (no portal loop by surprise), guideline 26.

### 5.7 Turnable arrows (beyond chambers 6 and 7 and 04a's ideas)

21. **The arrow as a firing post** (arrow twist). The only tile that sees the wall the route needs is an arrow; the subject can face only the arrow's way there, so it has to turn the arrow with its button or switch to aim. *From:* Portal 2D's own rules; Lolo's arrows don't stop shots either. *Check:* guideline 16 for both ways the arrow points, guideline 27 (the player sees it turn first).
22. **Funnel, then flip** (arrow combine). A room entered through a fixed arrow; inside, a turnable arrow held by a button opens the way back only while held, so the box has to stay on the button, and the route must not need it afterwards. *From:* Lolo. *Check:* guideline 28 (reach the turnable arrow from plain floor), guideline 29, guideline 5 (no box needed beyond the valve).

### 5.8 Rays and mirror boxes (beyond chambers 8 and 9 and 04a's ideas)

23. **Mirror box shown in place** (mirror box teach). The ray hits a mirror box resting one tile off, which turns it into a wall; moving the box one tile turns it onto the receiver. *From:* Q.U.B.E. 2. *Check:* guideline 31 (every place the box can rest), guideline 19 (no switch where the subject stands to put it down).
24. **Mirror first, then needed elsewhere** (mirror box test). The mirror box lights the receiver that opens door 1, and then is needed on the button that opens door 2; the portal pair has to take over the ray. *From:* Portal 2's Laser Stairs. *Check:* guideline 31, guideline 30.
25. **Two jobs at the bend** (mirror box twist). The same as 24, except a button sits on the ray's corner tile: one mirror box can turn the ray and hold the button at once. Make another corner look like the obvious one. *From:* the Talos tripod needed twice. *Check:* rays pass buttons and a resting mirror box presses one (`docs/elements.md`); keep only one box in the chamber.
26. **Toggle between two receivers** (mirror box twist). Without the mirror box the ray reaches receiver A (door 1); with it, receiver B (door 2). The route needs both doors in turn, so the box goes down, comes up and goes down again. *From:* Zelda's crystal switch (via Boss Keys), rebuilt. *Check:* guideline 30 for both rays, guideline 31.
27. **A portal keeps what a door gave briefly** (ray and door combine). While a button holds door D open, the only line to wall W runs through it: put the box on the button, shoot a portal onto W, take the box to a second button and let D close. A portal in the ray then sends it out beyond D into the receiver. *From:* the Talos connector puzzle, rebuilt. *Check:* W isn't the tile right behind D; guideline 30; what the subject itself can reach through the same portal pair (guideline 14).
28. **Relay with a detour** (ray twist). Receiver R1 switches on emitter E2, whose ray opens the exit through R2; the subject's only route crosses the first ray, which would darken the whole chain. The answer routes the first ray through a portal pair so it skips the route. *From:* Talos connectors; Laser Maze checkpoints. *Check:* guideline 30 (no ray crosses what its own receiver holds), guideline 4 (one receiver per element); the macro solver refuses chambers with receivers, so check by hand (guideline 38).
29. **A run of four** (mirror box sequence across 3–4 chambers). One mirror box turns the ray onto a receiver; then the corner tile is a button; then two receivers; then a chamber that looks like the third but needs the portal pair instead, because the mirror box is needed on a button out of line. *From:* Blow's partition run. *Check:* each chamber on its own as above; the fourth is the one that breaks the habit.

### 5.9 Combine across elements

30. **Filters** (combine, late). One chamber where the subject, the box, a shot and the ray each reach a different region: an open ditch passes rays and shots but not the subject; a fizzler passes the subject but not the box or a shot; a light barrier passes nothing. The puzzle is ferrying each across its own barrier. *From:* Talos 2's driller and exclusion field. *Check:* guideline 22 (if slides are involved), guideline 30, and the solver's refusal of chambers with receivers.
31. **The capstone** (finale). Portal's TC18 introduces nothing and combines nearly everything; Valve's finale was "about the easiest puzzle in Portal". Without time pressure, Portal 2D's last chamber needs synthesis and spectacle: several elements working at once, a visible chain of doors with lit antlines, and a catch no harder than the run before it.

## How this was gathered, and its limits

- **Method.** Nine research passes ran in parallel, one per family of games and two on generic writing, each reading developer commentary, talks (from YouTube captions: about 70 videos), postmortems, interviews, papers and forum threads, and writing notes with a link per claim. The passes listed about 420 source entries (some appear in more than one pass) and opened about 350. The notes were then condensed into this document, which links its claims inline.
- **Spot check.** Sixteen key quotes were re-read at their source: fourteen exact, one a close paraphrase (now tightened), and one wrong label (Game Informer names only "checklisting"; its second kind of training has no name), now fixed. The developer commentary transcript doesn't number Portal's chambers; the numbers follow the Portal Wiki's grouping.
- **Gaps.**
  - Some talks couldn't be transcribed (YouTube rate limits): the GDC 2012 Portal 2 talk, the Hitman GO postmortem, Jolie Menzel's GDC 2016 puzzle workshop, Traynor's Wookash podcast. Written reports of them were used instead.
  - Blocked pages: Ido Tal's Portal: The Flash Version retrospective (search snippets only), the Valve Developer Community wiki (archived copies used), Rock Paper Shotgun.
  - No designer interview exists for Eggerland/Lolo, none was found for Snakebird's level design, and nothing on Pokémon's ice puzzles.
  - Writing on top-down portal games is thin.
  - One research pass ran out of web searches near its end, so symmetry and composition in puzzle layouts, and learned helplessness, are barely covered.
- **What isn't checked.** The chamber ideas in Part 5 are prose sketches: none was built, solved or tested. Several outside findings come from 3D or real-time games and are translated here; the translations are this document's reading of `docs/elements.md`.
