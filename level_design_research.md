## Part 3 — What the advice says, topic by topic

Tips for designing Portal 2D's chambers, by topic. Some repeat `docs/level_design.md`; the table above lists the ones it lacks that are concrete enough to become guidelines.

### 3.1 Core design workflows: how to build a chamber from scratch

**Pick the workflow for the job.**

- _Finding ideas:_ play with the rules until something surprising happens, then turn it into a chamber.
- _Building a chamber:_ start from the one interaction the solution must contain and work backwards (the steps below).
- _Teach chambers, and chambers meant to be worked out before the first move:_ place each piece in the order the player will notice it, so each deduction forces the next (the only face past the ditch that takes portals is in this column → shoot it from this column → this column is reached only carrying the box → put the box down first). After each edit, forget the answer and solve it fresh, asking whether a player who misses a move can make progress elsewhere; check each placement with the solver.
- _Multi-step chambers:_ work back from the exit, charting what each step needs; aim for a diamond (one solve opens two or three paths that rejoin).
- _Never_ make a chamber hard by taking a solved state and shuffling it: the difficulty comes out arbitrary.

**Building a chamber, step by step.**

1. **Write the chamber's idea as one sentence before placing a tile**: the move that, once known, makes the chamber trivial. If you can't write it, the chamber isn't focused.
2. **Build the smallest room where that move is the only way through**, with no needless walking.
3. **Play it as an adversary: for each shortcut, add exactly one restriction and write down what it blocks.** Delete any restriction that blocks nothing: it has become a red herring.
4. **Run the noise test**, at least twice per chamber: describe the solution and remove every tile and element you didn't mention. Start with none; keep a piece the solution doesn't mention only to block a shortcut (noted as in step 3), to stop an accidental solve, to change the pace, or to set up a lure (Part 4.1, pattern 2). A lure that wouldn't mislead anyone is clutter.
   - _Trim the possibilities too:_ fewer things to do that aren't the solution, more empty space, no tight box shuffling. Every plain wall face takes portals, so each is a possibility: make the faces the idea doesn't need blocked walls with no link. But a lone portal face among steel reads as one that must be used (3.8).
5. **Find the variations.** For each chamber, try an easier version, a harder one, an extra constraint, and a different element enforcing the same constraint. The cheapest swap is another barrier in the same place, since each lets different things through: an open ditch or a laser that is on stops the subject but not shots or rays; a fizzler that is on lets the subject through but closes portals, destroys a carried box and stops shots; a light barrier that is on stops the subject, shots and rays (Part 5, idea 4). The easier versions become teach chambers. Variants in a row build a habit to break (Part 4.1, row 10); spread across runs, each arrives fresh.

**Where ideas come from on demand.**

- _The pair table:_ the interaction table in `docs/elements.md` puts every element against every other. Put a pair in an empty room and play until something surprising happens, then build backwards; the surprising cells are chamber candidates.
- _Completeness, then surprise:_ for each element, ask how it behaves with every other ("on a slide floor? in a ray? behind a fizzler?"), then cut the cases a player would predict, keeping those a tutorial needs.
- _Count an element's ideas before planning its run:_ list its distinct consequences (its cells in the interaction table, plus triples, which give the least predictable results). A run with more chambers than entries repeats a trick, so an element may not support a full run of 10–12 chambers (guideline 41).
- _Unintended solutions and near-misses:_ turn an unintended solution (one the solver turns up, or your own) into its own chamber, and a plan that almost worked into a chamber where it does. This works where the layout made the attempt fail (a face that wouldn't take the portal, a box one tile out of the ray, a slide that stopped a tile short); where a rule forbids it (shooting while carrying, shooting through a light barrier that is on), keep the failure as a lesson.
- _Other strategies for a small grid:_ list action sequences (shoot then carry; carry, put down, shoot, pick up); try extreme geometry (every wall takes portals, or only one does); start from a solvable chamber and keep changing it until it's impossible, then undo the last change, to find its tightest version; use elements as gadgets (an arrow as a one-way valve, a switch as a latch); translate puzzles from other games.
- _Park ideas:_ keep a list of ideas that have no chamber yet and go back to it; an idea can wait months for its chamber.

**Expect waste.** Plan to keep a third to a half of what you draft: about 20–30 drafts for the 10 chambers still to make. Cut as you go, the weakest first when a better one arrives: a chamber that repeats another, or one whose route is a list of separate small jobs (open this door, cross that ditch, press that button) with no single moment, since problems that split into parts are easy (3.4). Tag every draft, kept or cut, by its elements and role, so the pool can be searched later.

**Keep a card per chamber**: its sentence, its elements, its role (teach, test, twist, combine or rest), what the player must already know, and:

- _Its purpose_: teaching, reinforcing, a memorable moment, or a rest between hard chambers (3.4). If you can't name one, rework or cut the chamber.
- _Its place and difficulty, re-read at every draft_: a chamber that outgrows its card moves later or splits in two. Laid side by side, the cards show gaps, such as a chamber that suddenly adds four new elements.
- _Its intended route in five or six key steps_ (box onto B1; blue portal on W from B1; box to B2; cross; exit). The recorded solution breaks on any layout edit, so while a draft changes, check after each tweak that the route still passes its key steps, and record the presses once the layout settles.
- _Its lure and its sticking point_: what a player will likely try first (3.5) and where they should get stuck.

**Tentpole first.** For a run, design its combine chamber first, list the few things in it players won't understand yet, and derive the teach, test and twist chambers that plant them.

### 3.2 Mistakes to avoid

**Tedium.**

- _Walking is not thinking._ Long walks add tedium, not difficulty. A choice among a few options (which button gets the box, which face gets the portal) whose result only shows after a walk will be tried one by one: show the result where the choice is made (an antline lighting, a door in view).
- _No grind after the insight._ Once the idea has clicked, don't make the player carry it out through a long move sequence: replaying a solved chamber should take a minute at most.
- _No filler._ Don't run several easy chambers on the same thing; cut a series to its best few.

**Unfair dead ends** (Portal 2D has no undo, so these weigh more).

- _Deal with every dead end, in order of preference_: design dead ends out; make them visibly fatal at once; keep the replay short. A dead end costs the time before the player notices it plus the restart, and a hidden one teaches nothing. Restart sends the player back to the start, so each failed try replays every move before it: put the step the player should experiment with near the start of the route, or keep the moves before it few.
- _The catch early, nothing new late._ When a chamber's steps must come in a fixed order, put the catch in the first step, make every later step plain, and allow no dead end or new trick after the key move. A hard step the player can take in any order costs less than one fixed late.
- _Recovery built in._ In box-and-door chambers, players portal the box past the door and trap themselves with buttons but no box (the guide's "A needed box out of reach"). A destroyed box returns to its start and a switch sends a box home, so a switch reachable from where a box might get stranded is a rescue path. Once an area is solved, a short way back to its box spares a player who tried something.
- _Fear of failure kills experimenting._ Keep chambers short enough that a restart isn't a punishment. A switch can't be undone and an arrow is a one-way step, so keep both off the tiles a player crosses while looking around, and let walks and shots be tried before anything is committed.

**Clutter and noise.**

- _Every element must matter._ If one box gives a chamber away, the fix is a better idea, not a second box. Extra pieces are busywork that every Restart replays. When every tile has a purpose, players trust the chamber; once they meet a decoy, they hunt for decoys everywhere.
- _Clutter creates shortcuts, not difficulty._ Before adding an element to hide the answer, ask what simpler solution it enables.
- _No unreachable bait._ A box, button or switch in a sealed area reads as a target, and players will keep trying to reach it.
- _No overload._ Players give up before trying a chamber with too much to take in at a glance. Aim for 2–4 kinds of element per chamber, each used at least twice.
- _Would a door do the same?_ Two near-pairs: a closed door and a light barrier that is on both stop walking, shots and rays; a laser that is on and an open ditch both stop walking and let shots and rays through. They differ mostly in placement (a light barrier or a laser can lie over slide floor and runs between end plates; the walls facing a door never take portals). For each element in a draft, ask what only it does there, and use the plainer one when the answer is nothing.
- _Variety belongs to the campaign, not the chamber._ A combine chamber makes its elements act on each other; a box room, then a slide room, then a ray room in one chamber is three chambers.

**Unintended shortcuts.** Rework a chamber when players skip chunks of it too easily; a skip that takes more skill than the intended route can stay. Close a shortcut by taking away what makes it possible (a wall tile, a blocked wall in place of a face that takes portals, a moved box start, slide floor made plain), not by punishing it (a fizzler across it, an arrow into a pocket), which also hurts honest players who wander there. Two holes to check in every draft:

- _Shooting through an open door._ Shots pass an open door, so "stand on the button, shoot through the doorway, skip the box" is open in every box-and-door chamber unless the line is broken. Decide per chamber whether it's the lesson or a cheat.
- _A walking route that works will be taken._ In a turn-based game a slower route costs nothing, so making it longer steers no one. Close it.

**Nothing should look like a timing check.** Players who suspect reflexes once suspect them everywhere. Slides and walk-outs animate, so never lay out a chamber where a slide looks like it has to be beaten or caught in time.

**Intended oddities read as bugs.** Several rules look like bugs at first: the portal keys silent inside a fizzler that is on, a slide loop refused, a switch refusing a put-down, a button slid over unpressed. A chamber that uses one on purpose should show it first where nothing is at stake (3.3, "Show it before requiring it").

**Wrong lessons.** Watch for a chamber that teaches something false or punishes the right move: a button meant for the subject sends players looking for a box that isn't there.

### 3.3 Introducing mechanics without text

- **First contact is forced, safe and unambiguous**: a room where the only thing to do is the new move, with the reward just beyond.
- **Gate understanding, so stumbling fails.** Lay out a teach chamber so wandering around doesn't solve it. If a player can still stumble into the answer, the next chamber must check the idea.
- **One key move early, several solutions later.** A run's teach and test chambers need one key move, not one route: routes may differ in which tile a shot is fired from, but a copy of the chamber with the key move made impossible (its wall face set to refuse portals) should come back unsolvable (candidate 11). Its twist and combine chambers may allow several solutions. A trivial unintended route early can stay if it still teaches something new.
- **Show it before requiring it.** Demonstrate a consequence on something unimportant first: a box the player doesn't need, lying where a door closes or a fizzler switches on, shows destruction and return before the player's own box is at stake; a mirror box already resting in a ray shows the turn.
- **Make the player stop where it matters.** A held key repeats and a slide carries the subject past, so where a chamber shows something the player must notice, make the route stop in front of it (a door to open, a button to press, a slide that ends facing it).
- **Correct the likely wrong belief.** When players are likely to assume something false (such as a portal's far end staying put), give them a chamber that forces the opposite.
- **Show that what looks lost isn't.** With no undo, a player who thinks they've lost presses Restart and learns a wrong rule. Likely false losses: a box breaking up (it flies home), both portals fizzling (shoot again), a portal vanishing as a blocked wall shuts, a door closing behind a portal that still leads back. Before a chamber relies on the player carrying on after one of these, give a chamber that shows it can be undone (Part 5, idea 12).
- **Teach the moves the controls hide.** Turning in place with the turn key is the only way to face a free tile without stepping onto it, and so to put a box down there (guideline 8; the Clover corridor, Part 5, idea 5, depends on it); shooting through a door held open is another (Part 5, idea 3). A line in the controls legend isn't a lesson: give each such move a chamber where it is the only way, before any chamber relies on it.
- **Consistent cues build habits.** Once early chambers always pair a button with a box, a button meant for the subject alone breaks the habit: good for a twist, confusing in a teach.
- **Take the training wheels off at the end of a run**: the run's last chamber drops the cue that pointed at the move, so the player sees it works anywhere.
- **Re-teach after a big new element.** Players forget an earlier trick once a big new element arrives; bring the trick back just before a chamber needs it again.
- **Fix the sightline, not the text.** A narrowed opening says "use me" where an open wall says nothing: one gap in a fizzler run, one portal face among blocked walls. When a choice doesn't matter to the idea (which side of a fizzler to shoot from, which portal first), make every option work, so no one stalls on it. Make each rule visible when it fires; the fizzler splash, the light barrier reaction and the miss ripple already do.
- **Lead without seeming to lead.** Steer with the layout, not text: put the first thing to use in view of the start, and let sightlines and antlines point at the next step.
- **Show the wiring.** The player shouldn't have to step on a button to learn what it does; the puzzle isn't working out how the room is wired. Drop an antline only where finding the link is meant to be the puzzle. Anything the player must keep in mind that doesn't help solve the chamber is noise: a link found without an antline must be remembered for the rest of the chamber, and so must a box's start tile, which nothing marks once the box has moved (guideline 6). Unless remembering is the puzzle, draw the antline, or let the player watch the box fly home before a route depends on it.
- **Don't drag.** One teach chamber per element, then move on: too much teaching bores, too little loses players.
- **Show chambers.** A trivial chamber that just shows a consequence can delight; not every chamber needs a challenge. But players who expect a puzzle hunt for one, and nothing in the game tells them otherwise, so a show chamber must look as simple as it is: few elements, the answer the first thing in view. A joke chamber (4.1, row 19) risks the same hunt.
- **Put the lesson next to its use**: the chamber that teaches something comes right before the one that applies it.

### 3.4 Difficulty and pacing

**What makes a chamber hard.**

- _Not size, not length._ The number of states doesn't predict how long players take, and the length of the shortest route only weakly. What does: moves that take things away from their goal, and above all how often the route has to switch between the chamber's parts (a box, a portal) and go back to one already started. One compact room where the route weaves between two boxes takes far longer than two rooms solved one after the other. The solver's fewest moves counts walking, which adds nothing; the nearer measure is the number of walk-then-one-action steps on the shortest route.
- _One key move per chamber is plenty._ Players spend most of their time before the hard step and finish quickly after it, and each extra bottleneck adds to it.
- _Gem, flow or slog._ Mark each chamber's card as a gem (one hard step, a short route around it) or a flow (several easy steps and no catch, such as a rest or a show chamber). A long route with more than one hard step is a slog: cut a step or split the chamber.
- _One hard part, the rest turned down._ Name the one thing that makes a chamber hard and keep the rest plain: few kinds of element, a short route, sub-goals in any order. Designers spot difficulty that comes from the idea but underrate difficulty from the layout, such as length and a fixed order.
- _Presentation is a dial._ The same puzzle gets much harder or easier by how it's shown. The dials: whether the player builds the setup or only triggers it (a mirror box already in the ray, 4.6 pattern 2), where boxes start (one starting in a ray shows that it stops rays), how many faces take portals, and whether the spot where the setup is made looks onto its target. Turn each towards hidden for a harder chamber, towards shown for a teach chamber. Start positions matter a lot: when a chamber turns out too hard, try moving a box start a tile or two before redesigning, and recheck guidelines 5–7, since a box's start is also where it lands when sent home.
- _Rays and mirrors:_ difficulty comes from the reflections and beam crossings in the solution, hardly from the number of emitters and receivers.
- _Count solutions, steps and options._ Every extra route the solver turns up makes a chamber easier, not just untidier. More steps make it harder, too many tedious. Too many options at each moment overwhelm; too few let players repeat whatever is in front of them until it works. Order chambers by which elements each needs the player to know.

**Make a chamber harder by tangling its subgoals, not by enlarging it**: one box needed on two buttons in turn; a portal placement for the first goal that breaks the second; a move that carries the box _away_ from its button first.

**Measuring difficulty with the solver** (not built):

- _Switch count:_ label each step that changes the chamber by the thing it moves (each box, the blue portal, the orange portal), split those things into two groups, and find the route that switches between groups the fewest times; the score is that count for the best split (in the research, an easy level scored 2 and a hard one 7). It was the research's best measure for ranking levels by difficulty. It's a cheapest-first search over pairs of state and last group used, where a switch costs 1 and any other step 0, which the solver's cost queue already supports.
- _Simulated player_ (for a chamber the solver can search to the end; the research's best predictor of solving time): random walks over the state graph that favour steps towards the exit, more strongly as it nears; a backward pass from the completed states gives each state its distance to the exit (the "Dead ends" idea in `docs/solver.md`), and a few thousand walks give a score. Let a walk enter a dead state now and then and charge it a restart: a chamber whose score jumps has costly dead ends.

**You'll grow blind to your own chamber's difficulty**, even with the solver's numbers in front of you: expect each chamber to be harder for a player than it feels to you.

**Pacing: a sawtooth, not a ramp.** Introduce each element alone in a simple chamber, practise it, combine it, let complexity rise, then reset with the next element. A steady ramp makes the last third exhausting.

**Rests.** Players tire after several hard chambers in a row: put a short, easy chamber between hard ones, such as a show chamber (3.3). Easy needn't mean big (guideline 36).

**Keep something new coming.** Puzzles alone start to feel dry after 15–30 minutes, and Portal 2D has no story to carry the player: have something new arrive at least that often, a new element or a show chamber.

**The finale needn't be the hardest.** The last chamber needs synthesis and spectacle, not the hardest catch; a very hard required finale is resented.

**Keep the hardest challenges where nothing depends on them.** Every chamber is in the required list and later ones assume earlier ones were learned. Put the hardest challenge in the par block's fewest moves and portals, a second route in a twist or combine chamber, or a hard late chamber nothing after depends on. Make sure everyone gets what they need first; a subtle truth for attentive players must never gate the next chamber.

**Ordering is a skill of its own.** When a chamber asks too big a jump from the one before, insert a bridge: design the chamber "between" the two, or split a step that needs two new ideas into chambers needing one each (a chamber whose ray needs two turns, a mirror box and a portal pair, comes after chambers that each need one). Or change earlier chambers so they vaguely hint at the mechanic: vaguely, since an earlier chamber that rehearses the answer robs the hard one of its aha (3.5, "Plant fragments"). If a chamber is still too hard after a bridge, move it late, where nothing after depends on it. When two chambers' cards (3.1) say the same thing, cut or merge one.

### 3.5 Creating "aha" moments instead of trial and error

- **Assumption → catch → revelation.**
  - The goal must be obvious: the player works out how, never what. Show the exit and what holds it shut from the start.
  - The catch is two things that seem in direct conflict; the minimal case is a button that opens a door you can't reach while standing on it.
  - It resolves by changing the order, starting from somewhere else, or, best, a revelation: a non-obvious but logical consequence of the rules that joins the player's toolbox.
  - The best chambers lure the player into a wrong assumption that walks them into the catch. It gives them a place to start, builds their picture of the chamber, all but guarantees a first failure (so they feel stumped instead of stumbling into the answer), and focuses them on the catch. Weak chambers have a catch too easy to resolve, no assumption (so players stumble into the answer), or too little revelation (busywork).
  - Make the lure fail plainly. A near miss reads as nearly right: a slide stopping one tile short of the button, a ray ending one tile from the receiver, the only portal face one row off the line. Unless the near miss is the clue, make the lure fail by a clear margin or for a reason the game shows at once (a door shutting on the step off its button, a band flaring on a refused shot).
  - And soon. Finding out after 20 minutes that everything was wrong frustrates, most of all when failing means starting over. Show the wrong assumption wrong within a few moves, before the player has built much on it (candidate 7; the near-miss census in 3.7).
- **Make the catch provable.** Where the catch must be felt, keep the movable parts few (one box, few faces that take portals), so the player can rule out the obvious routes by reasoning; feeling sure it's impossible while knowing it isn't drives them on.
- **For a hard twist, break a belief several chambers trained**; for an easier one, a local assumption. Players stay stuck until they drop a limit they set themselves, and a trained one is harder to drop.
- **Truth, not hardness.** An aha must show something true about the rules, or it feels empty. Design for the pleasure of understanding, not the pride of beating something hard.
- **Let the rules set the chamber, not the designer.** A draft that needs a pile of one-off patches (3.1, step 3), such as a lone blocked wall tile on the exact face a shortcut uses, reads as the designer saying no. Prefer a fix the room's own shape explains; when patches pile up, rethink the room.
- **No one-off tricks.** A solution that works at only one moment, which players can't experiment towards, feels like a trick and teaches little. A technique used once leaves players suspicious in every later chamber.
- **Kill trial and error.** Keep the steps few but the odds of any one guess low; the dial is how many faces take portals. A route of two shots and a carry is quick to play but hard to stumble into when many faces take portals, while a room with one portal face teaches and doesn't test. Gate so that stumbling dead-ends. Rays are always on and invite wiggling into an accidental solve, which robs the player of understanding: put the insight where wiggling can't reach it, such as which wall must hold a portal or where a box must already be before a slide.
- **Don't make a refused action look possible.** Players repeat an impossible action when it isn't obviously impossible. Here those are refusals: shooting the wall behind an end plate (guideline 12), shooting across a light barrier as if it were a laser, shooting while carrying, putting a box down while on a switch (guideline 19). Each already shows a miss ripple or plays the error sound; where the layout makes one look possible, move the elements apart or give the shot another wall.
- **Plant fragments; don't hand over the idea.** A chamber just before that rehearses the answer cuts solve time but kills the aha. Plant its parts a few chambers earlier (the key move on its own, a side action repeated until it primes the idea, a layout that shows the whole space at a glance), so the player forgets them just long enough and puts them together alone.
- **Prime a forgotten old tool just before.** Players fixate on a new element and forget the tools they have. Priming an old tool the chamber needs is fine; handing over its catch isn't. The likely fixation is an animated element (a light barrier, a ray, a fizzler's field, 3.8): when the answer is a plain crossing, make the chamber's first step, or the chamber before it, need the same crossing in a plain room.
- **Same pieces, new consequence.** A twist chamber brings no new element; it shows a new consequence of the ones already known.
- **Difficulty from what's missing.** Leaving out something nearly every earlier chamber had is hard to notice, and so makes a strong twist.

### 3.6 Classic patterns and tropes

The full catalog, each pattern mapped to Portal 2D's elements, is in Part 4. The core ones: the catch (two goals in conflict), the lure (an easy earlier use primes a wrong move), both orders fail (interleave), the single key move, the counterintuitive move, the hidden consequence, prepare then trigger, a proxy for being in two places, the reprise (same layout, one change), the sequence that breaks its own pattern, and the alternate solution turned into its own chamber.

### 3.7 Validation

The recorded solutions, the replay check and the solver are the base. What to add:

- _Recheck after every change._ An edit that looks harmless can change the rules' outcome: a wall placed beside a fizzler turns its connectors and changes which walls take portals (guideline 12).
- _Prove the negative._ A designer shows a chamber has a good solution; only an exhaustive search shows it has no shortcut (guideline 38).
- _Concept-required check._ Tag each chamber with the moves it introduces, requires and wants players to try and fail, and check that the required moves appear in every solution: besides "remove the links to an element, expect unsolvable" (guideline 38), "solvable with zero portals?" and "solvable without picking up the box?".
- _Near-miss census._ List the dead states within one or two actions of the intended route: those are the mistakes players will make. Each should be visibly fatal or deliberately instructive. Judge a draft by what its likely wrong tries show: a wrong portal pair, a box on the wrong button or a slide that overshoots should end where the player can see what stopped it (a miss ripple, a band reacting, a door staying shut, an antline left unlit).
- _One-change sweeps._ One added object can lengthen a route a lot or flip a chamber between solvable and unsolvable. Script the solver over single changes (one wall face set to refuse portals, one wall tile added or removed, a box start moved one tile) and list which lengthen the route or remove an unintended shorter one; you judge which to keep.
- _Forbidden facts._ Keep a per-chamber list of facts that must never be possible (this face never takes a portal; this tile is never reached before the box is placed), which the solver could check.
- _Draw the graph._ For slides, every tile a slide can stop on is a node and every slide an edge; for links, the reachable state graph, where every change of state should be a real choice. Both show dead ends and false choices a sketch hides (guidelines 20, 24).

### 3.8 Player psychology

- **Players should blame themselves.** Warn of a danger and show a way out, and players try harder after failing; fail them without warning and they blame the game, and like it less.
- **Stress blocks learning.** In a turn-based game the stress is complexity: teach in a calm, small chamber before a combine chamber needs it. The first chamber that needs a new move holds no other new or hard element: the first mirror-box chamber isn't also the first light-barrier chamber.
- **Trust is a resource.** Decoys, ambiguous solutions, one-off tricks and inconsistent rules all spend it, and players push through "this seems impossible" only for a designer they trust. An answer that hinges on something no chamber hinted at makes players search every corner from then on. Write down the campaign's conventions, make each one plain to the player, and break none by accident: for example, whether every box in a chamber is needed (a spare box, 4.3 row 10, breaks that), and whether every link has an antline until a chamber plainly stops drawing them.
- **Everything visible reads as meaningful.** An odd niche is read as there for a reason; on a single-screen grid with no decoration, every tile shape is read as intent. Players also assume they have exactly what they need and that objects stay where they're useful: a box on a button whose door is open reads as done, so a chamber whose answer lifts it off again (4.1, row 5) should have no spare box to try first (4.1, row 11).
- **Symmetry makes every break in it read as meant.** A mirrored chamber is easier to take in and points at its one odd tile: put the break where the answer is, and check that a wall added only to stop a shortcut (3.1, step 3) isn't the loudest odd tile in the room.
- **Match what matters with what stands out.** Eyes go to movement and light: lasers, open portals and rays glow, fizzler fields drift and light barriers flicker, so an animated element is noticed before a still one, and a moving distractor pulls harder than a still one.
- **Fair and unfair red herrings.**
  - _Unfair:_ decoration that seems to matter, leftovers of removed puzzles, and a wrong approach that can't be proven wrong, the most discouraging of all. Add no decoys; players find their own. Lures built from real rules are fine (below).
  - _Fair:_ a lure built from real rules that the player's own reasoning produces (the assumption in 3.5), or several plausible uses of the same real elements, only one of which works. An unused stretch of portal wall is fine; a single portal face between steel walls, which looks like it has to be used, isn't.
  - _Fix:_ a wrong approach that fails at once and visibly is fair. When one doesn't, stop players trying it or make it fail visibly: within a few moves (a missed shot ripples, a fizzled box flies home, a door stays shut), or else close it off, for instance with a face that refuses portals.
- **Confirm progress.** Make each sub-goal show when it's reached: an antline turning amber, a door opening, a receiver lighting. Give a sure first sub-goal within the first few moves.
- **Separate independent sub-puzzles visibly**, or players take them for one big puzzle.
- **Let a solved part read as done.** The whole chamber stays on screen, so a finished part stays in view: keep the chamber compact, and let what is done look done (its door open, its antline lit), so the player can tell which elements still matter.
- **No hints towards a solution.** They tend to make players worse at solving; clearer chambers and earlier fragments work better. Words that say what an element is or does, once layout fixes have run out, are another matter: that is what `docs/hints.md` designs for the first chambers.

## Part 4 — Pattern catalog, mapped to Portal 2D

Patterns from the games and the generic writing, grouped by the Portal 2D element they rest on. Each row names the source, says how it's built with Portal 2D's elements, and adds a check against your rules where one applies. The mechanic-specific writing (ice puzzles, one-way tiles, laser mazes, switch logic) is summarised at the end of each section. Take one pattern per chamber: stacking patterns you admire is how J.B. Lewis's _Think Tank_ drifted from slot 39 to 133 (3.1, the card).

**Five facts about Portal 2D that change how outside patterns translate:**

1. **No AND gate.** Two buttons or receivers on one element close it when either is released or darkens (guideline 4). Portal 2's "several inputs on one door" becomes doors in series, or a receiver that switches on a second emitter whose ray reaches a second receiver.
2. **Links only open things when activated.** Pressing, using or lighting unblocks; releasing or darkening blocks again. "Press X to close Y" can't be built directly. Exclusive states have to come from layout: one box for two buttons, a mirror box steering a ray between two receivers, a turnable arrow, a switch that sends a box home (which releases its button).
3. **No hidden state, no timing, no momentum, no undo.** Every pattern that relies on fake walls, a clock, speed or an undo cushion is out. Slides are the only "keep moving" rule, and they resolve within one step.
4. **Carrying, not pushing.** Push-block deadlocks don't exist; a box is also a wall while it rests; full hands mean no portals.
5. **Shots pass open doors, lasers, ditches, boxes and arrows.** Most portal shortcuts come from this (guideline 14).

## Part 5 — Chamber ideas, by element and part

Prose sketches, not layouts: none has been built, solved or tested. Each names its part (teach, test, twist, combine, as in guideline 40), the pattern it comes from, and the guidelines to check when building it. They're grouped by element and slotted into neither plan.

What already exists, so the ideas avoid repeating it: chambers 4 and 5 teach and test slide floors, 6 and 7 teach and twist turnable arrows, 8 and 9 teach and test rays through portals, and stage 04a left six ideas (a fizzler mid-slide, a box carried across slides, an arrow held by a box, arrows changing where a slide ends, a ray crossing the tile before its own door, a mirror box carried around a fizzler). Gaps worth noting: **no chamber uses a light barrier or a mirror box yet**, and only chambers 1 and 3 use fizzlers.

### 5.1 Portals (basics)

1. **Gated islands** (teach or test). Three or four floor islands in a row, separated by lasers or open ditches, each with exactly one wall face that takes portals and sees the next island. The only way forward is a crossing per island, in order; a wrong shot is fixed by re-shooting. _From:_ Portal TC01's gating. _Teaches:_ aiming from the right tile, and that either portal can be the entrance. _Check:_ guideline 17 (each island has a face it can shoot from to come back), guideline 12 (no island's face is the wall at the end of a laser run).
2. **Shoot past what you can't walk past** (laser teach). A corridor to the exit cut by a run of lasers; one wall beyond the run takes portals, one wall on the subject's side does too. _From:_ Portal: The Flash Version's blue plasma wall. _Teaches:_ lasers block walking, not shots. _Check:_ the wall beyond must not be the end-plate wall of the laser run (guideline 12).
3. **Hold the door with a shot** (door teach, or a twist after box-and-button chambers). A button opens a door, and there is no box. Standing on the button, the subject faces the open doorway and a wall beyond it that takes portals; it shoots, steps off (the door closes), shoots the second portal on its own side and crosses. _From:_ Portal's TC11, which made a lesson of the move TC04 blocked as a cheat; also Flash Task 20. Players miss this move easily: with a floor button they search for a box, and even with a timed button they don't think to shoot through the open door. Portal 2D has neither a timed button nor a ticking cue (a switch opens a door for good, and nothing is timed), so the cue must come from the layout: no box anywhere in the chamber, the button tile looking straight through the doorway at the wall that takes portals, an antline from the button to the door. Expect players to hunt for a box. _Teaches:_ open doors pass shots; a door closing doesn't close portals. _Check:_ the far wall must not be the tile right behind the door (those faces never take portals); after this chamber, every box-and-door chamber must decide whether this move is allowed (Part 3.2).
4. **The missing rule** (light barrier teach by contrast, or laser twist). Two routes to the exit area: one cut by a light barrier run, one by a laser run, each with a wall beyond that takes portals. Shooting across the light barrier misses (the band reacts); across the laser it works. _From:_ Baba's level 7 ("difficulty from what is missing"). _Teaches:_ light barriers stop shots, lasers don't. _Check:_ the light barrier's own end-plate walls take no portals (guideline 12); give the light barrier no link, so it stays on.

### 5.2 Boxes, buttons and doors

5. **The Clover corridor** (box twist). A one-tile corridor: start, box, floor, button, floor, floor, exit, where the button opens the exit. Carrying the box to the button and putting it down from the start side seals the corridor with the exit open beyond it; the answer is to carry the box over the button, turn on the far side (turn key) and put it down facing back. _From:_ Stephen's Sausage Roll's _The Clover_. _Teaches:_ a box bars its tile; the put-down tile is the one faced. _Check:_ guideline 1 (button not next to the exit), guideline 9; it's fully reversible, which is why it suits a game with no undo.
6. **Hands full** (box test). The button sits in a room reached only by portal. Carrying the box, the subject can't shoot, so it must shoot first, or put the box down, shoot and pick it up again. Escalation: the only tile that sees the right wall is one the subject reaches while carrying. _From:_ the carry rule plus Snakebird's "too short, too long". _Check:_ guideline 18 (a tile to put the box down on wherever the subject can arrive carrying it).
7. **One box, two buttons in turn** (box combine). One box must hold button A (opens door A) and later button B (bridges a ditch or opens door B); taking it off A closes door A behind the subject, so the order and a portal placed while A was held decide the route. _From:_ Chip's _Teleblock_; for Jarušek and Pelánek's decomposition finding, the route must also go back to the box after placing a portal while A is held (measure it as in 3.4). _Teaches:_ nothing new; it's a test of planning. _Check:_ guideline 7 (box starts not on tiles where it could land under the subject), guideline 1.
8. **Counterintuitive first move** (box twist). The box starts beside its button, but putting it there first blocks the corridor to the only wall the subject needs to shoot. The answer carries it away, shoots, then comes back. _From:_ the counterintuitive-move metric. _Check:_ guideline 9 (a reachable tile beside the box wherever it rests).
9. **Prepare, then trigger** (switch combine). The exit opens only by a switch whose links also send both boxes home, so the buttons they held come up and their doors close; the player must have placed portals beforehand so the route to the exit survives. The antlines show what the switch will do. _From:_ Lolo's sleeping monsters. _Check:_ guideline 10 (switch, not button, sends boxes home), guideline 6 (box start tiles), guideline 7.

### 5.3 Fizzlers

10. **Set your portals first** (fizzler test). Box on one side of a fizzler that is on, button on the other. Walking the box through destroys it and closes both portals; the answer is to place both portals from a tile whose shot lines miss the field, then carry the box through the portals. _From:_ Portal TC09. _Check:_ guideline 13 (don't make the player stand in the field to aim), guideline 12 (the walls behind the connectors take no portals).
11. **Fizzle to respawn** (fizzler twist). The box starts on button B1, which holds door D1 open. The route needs the box elsewhere first (taking it closes D1), and later needs D1 open again from a side where B1 can't be reached (past an arrow). The answer: carry the box into the fizzler; it flies home onto B1 and D1 reopens. _From:_ the Portal 2 community's "fizzle to respawn"; Baba's _Tiny Pond_. _Teaches:_ a destroyed box goes to its start, wherever that is. _Check:_ guideline 6 (this is its "box delivered by its start tile" used on purpose), guideline 7 (the subject is never on B1 when the box lands), an antline from B1 to D1 so the effect reads.
12. **Cheap first failure** (fizzler teach). The obvious walk carries the box straight into a fizzler that is on: it breaks up and lands back at its start two tiles away, and both portals close. Nothing is lost but a few steps, and the way round (a portal pair placed first) is then plain. _From:_ Chip's _Tiny_ (an instant, cheap failure in a tiny room), Birdwell ("see other characters make mistakes"). A box can't _start_ on a fizzler, a door or a laser, so in Portal 2D the demonstration has to be one the player causes. _Check:_ guideline 5 (the start tile stays in reach), guideline 13.

### 5.4 Light barriers

13. **A wall you can switch** (light barrier teach). A room split by a light barrier run held off by a button and its box; the barrier blocks walking and shots, so neither the subject nor a shot crosses until the box is on the button. _From:_ first contact forced and safe. _Check:_ guideline 1 (button not next to the band), guideline 2.
14. **Barrier as a stopper** (slide and light barrier combine). A slide lane overshoots the tile beside the exit; a light barrier across the lane, held off by a receiver lit by a ray, comes on when the player puts a box in the ray, and the next slide stops in front of the band. _From:_ the Lara Croft GO expansion's eyes; Portal 2's _Turret Blocker_. Close to your own slide-bridge idea in `level_design/levels.txt`. _Check:_ guideline 30 (the ray must not cross the barrier's tile), guideline 20 (work out every stop with the band on and off), guideline 31.

### 5.5 Ditches and blocked walls

15. **Ferry across islands** (portal and box combine). Islands separated by open ditches, crossed only by portals; the box must reach the last island. Each hop is put down, re-aim, pick up, cross. _From:_ Chip's _Oversea Delivery_. _Check:_ guideline 18 (a tile to put the box down on each island), guideline 17 (a way back from each island while it matters).
16. **Pull the rug** (ditch twist). A box rests on a bridged ditch, held by a button; releasing the button destroys the box, which flies home to a start tile beyond a barrier the subject can't carry it past. _From:_ Portal 2's _Pull The Rug_. _Check:_ guideline 1 (never under the subject), guideline 6 (the start tile is meant to be reached this way).
17. **Steel that opens** (blocked wall teach). The only wall that sees across the gap is a blocked wall, which takes portals only while a button holds it; the box goes on the button, the portal goes on the wall, the subject crosses. _From:_ Portal's staged tool; Quantum Conundrum's per-room battery. _Teaches:_ blocked walls refuse portals until a link opens them. _Check:_ guideline 2 (no side of the linked blocked wall reachable by crossing from the button), guideline 13 (its portal vanishes if the wall is blocked again).
18. **The portal budget** (blocked wall combine). One switch unblocks a group of walls, changing which faces take portals across the whole chamber; the player must choose when to use it, since switches can't be undone. _From:_ Ocarina's Water Temple via Boss Keys. _Check:_ guideline 4 (only the switch links those walls).

### 5.6 Slide floors (beyond chambers 4 and 5 and 04a's ideas)

19. **Box as a stopper** (slide twist). A long lane ends at a dead end; the exit (or a side door) is one tile before the end. Without the box the subject overshoots; with the box on the end tile it stops beside the door. Escalation: the only box is also needed on a button, so a portal has to take over one of its jobs. _From:_ Chip's blocks on ice; Ricochet's helper robots. _Check:_ guideline 20, guideline 24, and Kluiver's caution that free boxes make most of an open field reachable.
20. **Nine-square lattice** (slide and portal combine). A 3×3 lattice of plain stop tiles joined by slide lanes, some walled off; walls at lane ends take portals, so the player edits the graph. _From:_ Chip's _I Slide_; switch stations from _Blink_. _Check:_ guideline 24 (every stop tile has a move that leads somewhere new), guideline 25 (no portal loop by surprise), guideline 26.

### 5.7 Turnable arrows (beyond chambers 6 and 7 and 04a's ideas)

21. **The arrow as a firing post** (arrow twist). The only tile that sees the wall the route needs is an arrow; the subject can face only the arrow's way there, so it has to turn the arrow with its button or switch to aim. _From:_ Portal 2D's own rules; Lolo's arrows don't stop shots either. _Check:_ guideline 16 for both ways the arrow points, guideline 27 (the player sees it turn first).
22. **Funnel, then flip** (arrow combine). A room entered through a fixed arrow; inside, a turnable arrow held by a button opens the way back only while held, so the box has to stay on the button, and the route must not need it afterwards. _From:_ Lolo. _Check:_ guideline 28 (reach the turnable arrow from plain floor), guideline 29, guideline 5 (no box needed beyond the valve).

### 5.8 Rays and mirror boxes (beyond chambers 8 and 9 and 04a's ideas)

23. **Mirror box shown in place** (mirror box teach). The ray hits a mirror box resting one tile off, which turns it into a wall; moving the box one tile turns it onto the receiver. _From:_ Q.U.B.E. 2. _Check:_ guideline 31 (every place the box can rest), guideline 19 (no switch where the subject stands to put it down).
24. **Mirror first, then needed elsewhere** (mirror box test). The mirror box lights the receiver that opens door 1, and then is needed on the button that opens door 2; the portal pair has to take over the ray. _From:_ Portal 2's Laser Stairs. _Check:_ guideline 31, guideline 30.
25. **Two jobs at the bend** (mirror box twist). The same as 24, except a button sits on the ray's corner tile: one mirror box can turn the ray and hold the button at once. Make another corner look like the obvious one. _From:_ the Talos tripod needed twice. _Check:_ rays pass buttons and a resting mirror box presses one (`docs/elements.md`); keep only one box in the chamber.
26. **Toggle between two receivers** (mirror box twist). Without the mirror box the ray reaches receiver A (door 1); with it, receiver B (door 2). The route needs both doors in turn, so the box goes down, comes up and goes down again. _From:_ Zelda's crystal switch (via Boss Keys), rebuilt. _Check:_ guideline 30 for both rays, guideline 31.
27. **A portal keeps what a door gave briefly** (ray and door combine). While a button holds door D open, the only line to wall W runs through it: put the box on the button, shoot a portal onto W, take the box to a second button and let D close. A portal in the ray then sends it out beyond D into the receiver. _From:_ the Talos connector puzzle, rebuilt. _Check:_ W isn't the tile right behind D; guideline 30; what the subject itself can reach through the same portal pair (guideline 14).
28. **Relay with a detour** (ray twist). Receiver R1 switches on emitter E2, whose ray opens the exit through R2; the subject's only route crosses the first ray, which would darken the whole chain. The answer routes the first ray through a portal pair so it skips the route. _From:_ Talos connectors; Laser Maze checkpoints. _Check:_ guideline 30 (no ray crosses what its own receiver holds), guideline 4 (one receiver per element); the macro solver refuses chambers with receivers, so check by hand (guideline 38).
29. **A run of four** (mirror box sequence across 3–4 chambers). One mirror box turns the ray onto a receiver; then the corner tile is a button; then two receivers; then a chamber that looks like the third but needs the portal pair instead, because the mirror box is needed on a button out of line. _From:_ Blow's partition run, which slips in the piece its last panel turns on two panels earlier: so let an earlier chamber of the four use the portal pair to carry the ray where nothing depends on it. _Check:_ each chamber on its own as above; the fourth is the one that breaks the habit.

### 5.9 Combine across elements

30. **Filters** (combine, late). One chamber where the subject, the box, a shot and the ray each reach a different region: an open ditch passes rays and shots but not the subject; a fizzler passes the subject but not the box or a shot; a light barrier passes nothing. The puzzle is ferrying each across its own barrier. _From:_ Talos 2's driller and exclusion field. _Check:_ guideline 22 (if slides are involved), guideline 30, and the solver's refusal of chambers with receivers.
31. **The capstone** (finale). Portal's TC18 introduces nothing and combines nearly everything; Valve's finale was "about the easiest puzzle in Portal". Without time pressure, Portal 2D's last chamber needs synthesis and spectacle: several elements working at once, a visible chain of doors with lit antlines, and a catch no harder than the run before it.
