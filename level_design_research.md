## Candidate additions to the guide

Points marked **[new]** in Part 3 that are concrete enough to become guidelines in `docs/level_design.md`. Nothing here has been added to the guide.

| 2   | Keep a list of each restriction and what it blocks; one that blocks nothing is a red herring                                                                                                                                                                   |
| 3   | A box-and-door chamber must decide whether "stand on the button, shoot through the open door" is the lesson or a cheat, and break the line if it's a cheat                                                                                                     |

## Part 3 — What the advice says, topic by topic

Each topic gives the points the sources agree on, each sourced, then what it means for Portal 2D. The tag at the end of a point compares it with `docs/level_design.md`: **[covered: G#]** means your guide already says it (guideline number), **[new]** means it doesn't, **[tension]** means sources disagree or the point pulls against the guide.

### 3.1 Core design workflows: how to build a chamber from scratch

**"Forwards" and "backwards" mean five different things.** Read any advice with that in mind:

| #   | Workflow                 | What it means                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Who                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Verdict                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| --- | ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Exploratory forwards     | Play with the rules until something surprising turns up; package it as a puzzle. You don't know the answer when you start                                                                                                                                             | Blow and ten Bosch ([IndieCade 2011](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OGSeLSmOALU)), VanDevander ([talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0zVjdEhHmGo)), Brett Taylor ("the mechanics came up with the levels", [GDC 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B36_OL1ZXVM))                                                                                                        | Recommended for _finding_ ideas                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| 2   | Forward deduction chain  | Place each piece in the order the player will notice it, solving as you build, so each deduction forces the next; after each edit, clear the answer and test-solve from scratch, asking "what if the player doesn't see this move … can they make progress elsewhere" | Traynor ("Make a forward design chain": "place objects to create a sequence of deductions, such that the player also follows that exact same sequence of deductions", [cwpat.me](https://cwpat.me/misc/puzzle-level-idea-strategies/)); Grant, from logic puzzles: "quite tight control over the solution experience" ([part 3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsbfkMuaUxs)) | Recommended for teach chambers and for chambers meant to be worked out before the first move. Portal 2D suits it, since the whole chamber is on screen and nothing is hidden (fact 3), and its rules supply the steps: the only face past the ditch that takes portals is in this column → shoot it from this column (G26) → this column is reached only carrying the box → put the box down first (G18). Check each placement with the solver, since Grant's examples have no moving parts **[new]** |
| 3   | Backwards from the trick | Pick the one interaction the solution must contain, build the smallest room where it is the only way, then add one restriction per shortcut                                                                                                                           | Hazelden, Martin, Traynor ("by far the most common technique I used", GDC 2024), Teikari, Brown, Croteam                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Recommended for _building_ a chamber; the most common method among the designers researched                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| 4   | Backwards from the goal  | Start at the exit and ask what each step needs, as a dependency chart; aim for a diamond shape (one solve opens two or three, which reconverge)                                                                                                                       | Ron Gilbert ([dependency charts](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)), Noah Falstein ([report](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-technique-lucasarts-used-to-design-its-classic-adventure-games)), Demon Arisen (central concept as the final move)                                                                                                      | Recommended for multi-step chambers                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| 5   | Backwards as obfuscation | Take a solved state and shuffle it until it's hard                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | VanDevander names it to warn against it: difficulty becomes arbitrary                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Avoid                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |

**The method most of the designers converge on**, in order:

1. **Write the one moment the chamber is about as a sentence** before placing a tile. Brett Taylor calls it the "solution sentence", "the secret to a magic trick": if the player knew it, the level would be trivial ([GDC 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B36_OL1ZXVM)). Corey Martin: if you can't say what a puzzle is about, "it's probably not very focused" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/bonfire-peaks-explores-complex-emotions-through-puzzle-design)). Croteam started from "only the 'catch'" ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/the-talos-principle-interview-ps4s-very-own-first-person-philosophical-puzzler-that-is-not-portal)); Valve from one teaching goal per chamber ([Game Informer 2010](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber.aspx)). **[new]**
2. **Build the smallest room where that moment is the only way through.** "A good puzzle is almost always as small as it can be, with no unnecessary walking" (Hazelden, [comment](https://www.electrondance.com/?p=12655)); Traynor makes "the easiest possible version of a puzzle, that still features the interesting idea" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/patrick-s-parabox-)). **[covered in part: G36 "no larger than its puzzle needs"]**
3. **Play it as an adversary; for each shortcut add exactly one restriction, and write down what it blocks.** Teikari works backwards "locking up doors behind him" ([GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7zLwa4bztWs)); Silverman makes the draft impossible, then adds one interesting fix ([Tuts+](https://code.tutsplus.com/portal-2-level-design-from-initial-idea-to-finished-level--gamedev-5321t)). Delete any restriction that blocks nothing: it has become a red herring. **[new]**
4. **Run the noise test.** "Describe the solution of the puzzle and remove anything you didn't mention" (Taylor, who advises vetting every level "at least two times" and vetted his own four to a dozen times). Noise "makes puzzles artificially harder": "a seeing through noise experience", not puzzle solving. Taylor kept it only to change the pace or to stop an accidental solve: in one Limelight level players won by backing up without knowing what they'd done, so he added a loop, and "nobody solves accidentally because there is noise in this level". His rule: "start with none and then add it from there". Brown adds a second reason to keep a piece: a lure. A Lara Croft GO lizard is "not really part of the solution", but it's there "to trick you into making the wrong assumption about how the puzzle works" ([GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg)). So in Portal 2D a piece the solution doesn't mention stays only to block a shortcut (noted as in step 3) or to set up a lure (Part 4.1, pattern 2); a lure that misleads no tester is clutter. **[new]**
   - _Trim the possibilities too._ Traynor pinned a box in a cranny so there were "less things to do that aren't the solution", and preferred a version of another puzzle "with only three boxes and more empty space", with "no tight box finagling" ([GDC 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HAvS-RwkjdA)). In Portal 2D every plain wall face takes portals, so each is a possibility: make the faces the idea doesn't need blocked walls with no link. Mind the counterweight in 3.8: a lone portal face among steel reads as one that must be used. **[covered in part: G36]**
5. **Find the variations.** Traynor tried an easier version, a harder one and an extra constraint on one puzzle, "and now I have four good puzzles", and did this "with every puzzle I made" (GDC 2024). His list adds "a different mechanism to enforce the same constraint" ([cwpat.me](https://cwpat.me/misc/puzzle-level-idea-strategies/)). In Portal 2D the cheapest new mechanism is another barrier in the same place, since each lets different things through: an open ditch or a laser that is on stops the subject but not shots or rays; a fizzler that is on lets the subject through but closes the portals, destroys a carried box and stops shots; a light barrier that is on stops the subject, shots and rays (Part 5, idea 4). The easier variants are your teach chambers; this is how "a hard puzzle creates the easy ones it needs" (Croteam's two-jammers story, [Nordic Game 2016](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D0ErxdacTT8)). Variants can also be spread out instead of put in a row: five Limelight levels share one idea, yet each is "a totally unique challenge", "mostly because they're scattered … across the entire game"; "if these were in sequence the player would probably pick up on the pattern" (Taylor's "cheat to get extra solution sentences"). In a row, variants build a habit to break (Part 4.1, row 10); spread across runs, each arrives cold. **[covered in part: G42]**

**Where ideas come from on demand.**

- _The pair matrix._ Put every element on both axes of a table and treat each cell as a prompt: two elements in an empty room, play until something surprising happens, then build backwards (Brown, [Developing 9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=akeVPZLZejY); Taylor; Traynor). Portal 2D already has this table: the interaction table in `docs/elements.md`. Each cell names the rules that bring two elements together; the surprising ones are chamber candidates. **[new]**
- _Completeness, then surprise._ For each element, ask each property question of every other element ("what if this is on a slide floor? in a ray? behind a fizzler?"), then cut the cells a player would predict, keeping those a tutorial needs (Blow, IndieCade 2011). **[new]**
- _Count an element's ideas before planning its run._ Grant calls it a puzzle type's capacity: "How many can you create of this puzzle type before you start repeating the same tricks over and over again?" "Something like a portal gun may only really support 10 different unique Eureka moments": traversal, line-of-sight shots, a portal on the ceiling, momentum, moving one portal and leaving the other to get back ([part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)). Portal 2D has no ceiling and no momentum (fact 3). Before giving an element a run, list its distinct consequences (its cells in the interaction table, and triples, since "three or more will give you unique emergent Eurekas"); a run with more chambers than entries repeats a trick. **[tension]** with G41's full run of 10–12 chambers.
- _Testers' near-misses and unintended solutions._ "A sign of a promising puzzle game is when I can play test three levels and come away with ideas for 10" (Hazelden, [YagmanX](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RR9-5FyKJOM)); turn an unintended solution into its own chamber (Hazelden, Teikari, Traynor). Portal's team got "the inspiration for new puzzles" from "playtesters solving puzzles in ways that we hadn't previously considered" (Barnett, Swift and Wolpaw, [Game Developer 2008](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/thinking-with-portals-creating-valve-s-new-ip)). Traynor's "things almost happen" moments are the near-misses: a tester's plan that doesn't quite work becomes a chamber "where that thing that almost happened, actually happens" ([cwpat.me](https://cwpat.me/misc/puzzle-level-idea-strategies/)). In Portal 2D this works where the layout made the attempt fail (a face that wouldn't take the portal, a box one tile out of the ray, a slide that stopped a tile short); where a rule forbids it (shooting while carrying, shooting through a light barrier that is on), the failure is a lesson to keep. **[new]**
- _Traynor's list of 29 strategies_ ([cwpat.me](https://cwpat.me/misc/puzzle-level-idea-strategies/)), the most useful for a small grid: enumerate action sequences (shoot then carry, carry–put down–shoot–pick up); extreme geometry (every wall takes portals, or one does); "Start with a level that's possible to solve, and keep making changes until it's impossible, then undo your last change", which finds the tightest version; gadgets (arrows as one-way valves, a switch as a latch); translate puzzles from other games (Portal is his own example).
- _Sit on it._ Lavelle carried some level ideas for months before a level turned up for them ([podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4P2iC7YQu1k)); Blow found The Witness's rules only after coming back months later "with an open mind" ([Truth in Game Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C5FUtrmO7gI)).

**Expect waste.** The designers studied shipped a third to a half of what they made. Parabox shipped 364 puzzles and left "over 600 unused draft levels", some of them earlier versions of shipped puzzles ([GDC 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HAvS-RwkjdA)); Talos 2 made "the double number of puzzles" ([Thinky Games](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KbMIp3q2Tc0)); Blow kept "less than half" ([stream clip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ku64fkXIKzk)). For the 10 chambers still to make, budget about 20–30 drafts. Taylor cut as he went, "ripping out the bottom 30 percent" whenever a better level arrived; most of what he cut was redundant, or "didn't revolve around one moment": the player "would kind of chip away the solution" (GDC 2019). In Portal 2D, a chamber whose route is a list of separate small jobs (open this door, cross that ditch, press that button) is the first to cut; that matches the finding that problems which split into parts are easy (3.4). **[new]**

**Keep a card per chamber**: its sentence, the elements it uses, its role (teach, test, twist, combine) and what it needs the player to know already. Brown's "puzzle bible" made gaps visible ("level 4 suddenly adds four new elements") and turned each gap into a brief ([Developing 9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=akeVPZLZejY)); Hazelden tags every level by mechanic and "intro" so a pool of drafts can be searched later ([ThinkyCon 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98tmqN0bUGc)). Your 04b chamber plan table is most of this already. **[new]** What the sources add to the card:

- _Its purpose._ "For each level ask what is the purpose of this level and if you can't answer that question it is a road to mediocrity" (Taylor). His purposes are teaching, reinforcing, "a unique identifiable moment" and "a palette cleanser or just a break up pacing"; the last has no slot in G40's teach, test, twist and combine (3.4, Rests).
- _Its place and difficulty, re-read at every draft._ J.B. Lewis planned _Think Tank_ for slot 39 of 149; it ended at 133 after a run of "Oh! I loved this mechanism in Level X; let's include it here!" ([The Simple Things](https://forum.bitbusters.club/thread-91.html)). A chamber that outgrows its card moves later or splits in two.
- _Its intended route in five or six key steps_ (box onto B1; blue portal on W from B1; box to B2; cross; exit). Talos's test bot kept "just the logic of the solution", not the exact movements, so it survived edits to the level ([Ladavac, GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/the-talos-principle-underwent-15000-hours-of-playtesting-but-not-by-humans-interview/)). Portal 2D's recorded solution is the presses, which any edit but an antline's breaks (G37); while a draft still changes, check after each tweak that the route still passes through its key steps, and record the presses once the layout settles.
- _Its playtest hypotheses_: what a fresh player will try first (the lure, 3.5), where they should get stuck, and about how long it takes; afterwards, mark each held or not. "Game designs are hypotheses / Playtests are experiments" (Ambinder, 3.7); a lure nobody falls for, or a stall in the wrong place, is the finding.

**Tentpole first.** Valve's Matt Wood builds "my ultimate challenge for the player" first, finds the "three or four things in here which the players don't really understand", and designs the chambers before it to plant them ([Kiwi Talkz](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3dYAZxr2FbY)). Teikari chose Baba's final level in the first two months ([Gamelab 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8eTFea_UZcc)). For a short run of four, design the combine chamber first and derive teach, test and twist from it. **[new]**

### 3.2 Mistakes to avoid

**Tedium.**

- _Walking is not thinking._ Lara Croft GO cut its maze levels: "too much swiping required with too little decisions to make" ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/?p=65919)). Brett Taylor gave a level two switches, four combinations, and a long walk "because i wanted them to stop and think"; players "still brute forced it anyway", since nothing marked a wrong state as wrong, and he cut it "and several others like it" (GDC 2019). In Portal 2D, Restart is instant, so a choice among a few options (which button gets the box, which face gets the portal) whose result shows only after a walk will be tried one by one: show the result where the choice is made (an antline lighting, a door in view). Joshua Taylor and Ian Parberry count "box lines", not pushes, because long hallways are "not difficult, just tedious" ([paper](https://ianparberry.com/pubs/GAMEON-NA_METH_03.pdf)). **[covered in part: G36]**
- _Execution after the insight._ Portal 1's quitters left after the two hardest-to-execute puzzles ([Wolpaw](https://www.engadget.com/2011-03-13-valves-erik-wolpaw-on-portal-2s-difficulty-ps3-steam-features.html)); Talos 2's bar is a 30–60 second redo once you know the steps ([Thinky Games](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B3yoVxhld4Q)). A turn-based grid gets most of this for free; don't bring it back through long move sequences after the idea has clicked. **[new]**
- _Filler._ Hazelden "absolutely hate[s]" runs of "ten repetitive easy puzzles"; Taylor would cut his six-level series to three ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/developers-wont-hold-hand/); GDC 2019). **[new]**

**Unfair dead ends** (Portal 2D has no undo, so these weigh more here than in any game researched).

- _Hidden dead ends teach nothing._ A move that makes the level impossible "and it's totally nonobvious" just makes players "reset until they solve it" ([Sokobond post-mortem](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2mrqujss0Sk)). Players leave dead states fast _once they notice_: in Sokoban research 74% of states were dead but players spent only 14.5% of their time in them ([Jarušek and Pelánek](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/stairs2010-final.pdf)). So the cost of a dead end is the time before the player notices it, plus the restart. **[covered in part: G15 "a chamber that can be lost on purpose should make it obvious"]**
- _Three ways out, in order of preference_: design them out (Talos 2: "we thought about every puzzle where you could get stuck"); make them visibly fatal at once (Wilderplace's designer, via [Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/?p=16930)); make the reset cheap (Grant on a Hearthstone puzzle whose slow reset made experimenting "very tedious", [30 lessons, part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)). Portal 2D's Restart is instant, but it sends the player back to the start of the route, so each failed try costs a replay of every move before it. Grant's Zelda boss could only be tried at the end of a long dungeon: "The experimentation required all of this effort beforehand" (same). Put the step a chamber wants the player to experiment with near the start of its route, or keep the moves before it few. **[covered: G15–G18, "States to avoid"]**
- _The catch early, nothing new late._ Chip's Challenge levels have no checkpoint or undo either, and CC1 "hardly ever resorts to pulling out increasingly harder challenges as an extremely linear level progresses, nor do most of these levels punish players for making poor choices at a late point": Mix Up's hardest sokoban is in its first half, and Four Plex's challenges "became less ambiguous" as it went on (J.B. Lewis, [In Defense of Consistency](https://forum.bitbusters.club/thread-81.html)). A hard step the player can take in any order costs less than the same step fixed late. In Portal 2D, when a chamber's steps must come in a fixed order, put the catch in the first step, make every later step plain, and allow no dead end or new trick after the key move. **[new]** (G15 says to avoid unnoticed dead ends, not where hard steps go)
- _Recovery built in._ Valve added box dispensers so players "could always be supplied with the required tools", after finding that in "virtually any puzzle involving boxes and doors" players could "portal the boxes to the other side of the door, thereby trapping themselves in a room with buttons but no boxes" ([commentary, TC13](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)), the guide's "A needed box out of reach". Portal Stories: Mel opens a shortcut back to the cube once an area is solved ("we don't want people to be punished for trying things out", [commentary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zja9oCowbX4)). Portal 2D has two built-in recoveries: a destroyed box returns to its start, and a switch can send a box home. A switch reachable from where the box might get stranded is a rescue path. **[covered in part: G5, G10]**
- _Fear of failure kills experimenting._ Long death animations made Portal 2 co-op players "afraid to take risks" ([commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)); Juul found the punishment players resent is setback, being made to replay ([Fear of Failing?](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). Keep chambers short enough that a restart isn't a punishment. **[new]** Lewis praises CC1 levels that let players "run around without the fear of tripping some button or collecting some item too early", and blames its loss on making "every tile in the playing field" serve the solution (In Defense of Consistency): a **[tension]** with the noise test. Part of his case rests on Chip's Challenge's small view, which Portal 2D doesn't have; the part that carries over is commitment. A switch can't be undone and an arrow is a one-way step, so keep both off the tiles a player crosses while looking around, and let walks and shots be tried before anything is committed.

**Clutter and noise.**

- Every element must matter. Clutter "interfered with the portal training process" (Valve, [commentary, TC05](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)); Blow padded Braid levels with extra monsters, since "if I only have one monster in the level it's going to give away the puzzle … I need to have some more kind of red herrings", and then "the game got better and better the more of that stuff that I eliminated" ([IndieCade 2011](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OGSeLSmOALU)): if one box gives a chamber away, the fix is a better idea, not a second box; extra pieces are often "just busy work that will frustrate you when you need to reset the level", and the best puzzles are "so small with so few moving parts that you can't believe that it's not more simple to figure out" (Brown, [GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg)), which weighs more with no undo, since every Restart replays them; The Witness removed buttons and levers "because the players are looking for buttons and levers everywhere" ([noclip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YdSdvIRkkDY)); Puzzledorf's player "started to trust the puzzle" once he saw every cell had a purpose ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/reflections-on-puzzle-design-in-puzzledorf)). **[new]**
- _Clutter creates shortcuts, not difficulty._ Refraction's researchers found distractor pieces "often only serve to give players the tools to build a simpler solution" ([Smith, Butler and Popović 2013](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/smith2013qop.pdf)). Before adding an element to hide the answer, ask what simpler solution it enables. **[new]**
- _Unreachable bait._ Players who saw an object they couldn't reach would "thrash on it for a really long time" (Kim Swift, [CES interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iQOIYQJUA2o)); a box, button or switch in a sealed area reads as a target. **[new]**
- _Overload._ Teikari: levels "very easily become overwhelming as the number of initially-active rules increases" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-i-baba-is-you-i-s-delightfully-innovative-rule-writing-system)); Puzzledorf's testers quit a dense level before trying it: "It was not the difficulty that scared people off, but having too much information to take in at a glance." The Portal 2 community settles on 2–4 elements per chamber, each reused at least twice ([ThinkingWithPortals](https://thinking.withportals.com/forum/topic/puzzle-producing-process-t5983)). **[new]**
- _Would a door do the same?_ Ten Bosch cuts a mechanic when "all the consequences that it adds are already contained" in the others, and one "relatively less rich than the others … so that the game is more focused" ([IndieCade 2011](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OGSeLSmOALU)). Portal 2D has two near-pairs: a closed door and a light barrier that is on both stop walking, shots and rays; a laser that is on and an open ditch both stop walking and let shots and rays through. They differ mostly in placement (a light barrier or a laser can lie over slide floor and runs between end plates; the walls facing a door never take portals). So for each element in a draft, ask what only it does there, and use the plainer one when the answer is nothing. **[new]** (candidate 9 only asks that two similar elements shown together differ)
- _Variety belongs to the campaign, not the chamber._ Lewis faults levels with "room after room filled with completely different challenges that require the player to switch gears" (In Defense of Consistency). A combine chamber makes its elements act on each other; a box room, then a slide room, then a ray room in one chamber is three chambers. **[new]** (G40 doesn't say what a combine chamber combines)

**Unintended shortcuts.** "So much of puzzle design is about forcing the player to do one very specific thing" (Brown, Developing 9). Even veteran Refraction designers shipped levels with undesirable solutions (Smith et al.). Valve's policy: rework a chamber when players "bypass chunks of the puzzle too easily", keep a "ninja" skip that takes more skill than the intended route (commentary, TC14). **[covered: G38, "The puzzle is bypassed"]** And close a shortcut by taking away what makes it possible, not by punishing it: in Portal's TC07, testers rode the rails to the exit; killing them on touch was "too much of an over correction" that frustrated "even skilled playtesters", while beams of light in place of the rails "solved both problems" ([commentary, TC07](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)). In Portal 2D, taking away means a wall tile, a blocked wall in place of a face that takes portals, a moved box start or slide floor made plain; punishing would be a fizzler across the shortcut or an arrow into a pocket, which also hurts players on the honest route who wander there. **[new]** Portal 2D-specific holes the research turned up:

- _Shooting through an open door._ Shots pass an open door, so "stand on the button, shoot through the doorway, skip the box" (Portal's TC04 cheat) is open in every box-and-door chamber unless the line is broken. Valve blocked it with glass; Portal: The Flash Version made it the answer of Task 20. Decide per chamber which it is. **[new]** (guideline 14 lists what stops shots, but not this pattern)
- _A walking route that works will be taken._ In a turn-based game there's no time cost, so a slower route never steers anyone: Valve's slow-lift fix in Portal's TC15 doesn't translate. Close the route. **[new]**

**Mode confusion.** "Players assume inherently that there's no agility needed in puzzle games"; break that once and they suspect reflexes everywhere (Taylor, GDC 2019). Portal 2D is strictly turn-based, but slides and walk-outs animate: nothing should ever look like a timing check. **[new]**

**Intended oddities read as bugs.** Players need reassuring "that no, the game is operating correctly. Unlike most other modes of fiction, games can and do break down at times" (Foster on Half-Life 2 mod MINERVA's locked armouries, [Radiator Blog](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/p/level-with-me-adam-foster-part-2.html)). Several Portal 2D rules look like bugs at first sight: the portal keys silent inside a fizzler that is on, a slide loop refused, a switch refusing a put-down, a button slid over unpressed. The guide keeps them from happening by accident; a chamber that uses one on purpose should show it first where nothing is at stake (3.3, "Show it before requiring it"). **[covered in part: "Something looks broken", G13, G19, G21, G25]**

**Wrong lessons.** Teikari's "DOOR IS SHUT" made players think SHUT meant solid (GDC Plays); Valve's weighted button meant for the player sent testers "searching hopelessly for a box" (commentary, TC11). Watch for "accidentally giving negative reinforcement for the right thing to do" (Swift, [Game Developer 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-inclusive-design-of-kim-swift)). **[new]**

### 3.3 Introducing mechanics without text

- **First contact is forced, safe and unambiguous.** Miyamoto's mushroom bounces off the first pipe back into the player ([Iwata Asks](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-New-Super-Mario-Bros-Wii/Volume-1/4-Letting-Everyone-Know-It-Was-A-Good-Mushroom/4-Letting-Everyone-Know-It-Was-A-Good-Mushroom-210863.html)); Puzzledorf's first tutorial is a single forced path ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/tutorial-design-in-puzzledorf-reflections)); Mega Man X traps the player in a pit where the only thing to do is the new move, with a reward just outside ([Sequelitis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8FpigqfcvlM)). **[covered: G40 teach]**
- **Gate understanding, so stumbling fails.** Valve rebuilt TC01 so "stumbling around will virtually always lead to a dead end" (commentary, TC01). **[tension]** with Traynor, who is "okay with people totally stumbling upon a solution", "so long as they properly grappled with that same concept in the immediately following puzzles" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/patrick-s-parabox-)); he inserts puzzles to "give someone a second chance to learn something if for example they stumbled into the solution in the first instance" (GDC 2024). Blow's first partition panel has "only four solutions … and two are right so you actually can get this one by brute forcing", and in the same talk ten Bosch states the condition: a Braid puzzle that "can pretty much be figured out by accident" is "okay … because there'll be later puzzles that … talk about the same thing" ([IndieCade 2011](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OGSeLSmOALU)). All agree on it: a stumble is fine only if the next chamber checks the idea.
- **One solution early, several later.** "For training purposes, there's generally just one correct solution to these early puzzles"; later puzzles open up (Faliszek, [commentary, TC04](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)). In Portal 2D: a run's teach and test chambers allow one route, its twist and combine chambers may allow several. **[new]** (guideline 40 asks only that a teach chamber's mechanic be the only way forward), but **[tension]**: Teikari let unintended solutions stand early, because "even the unintended, more trivial solutions often contain some element that's new to the player", and restricted them later ([Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-hempuli-oy-s-i-baba-is-you-i-)).
- **Unique key move, free route.** What must be unique is the key move, not every step: "make the key move or sort of the key step that you have to do provably unique"; later steps may branch, which "doesn't spoil the aesthetics of the puzzle having a unique key move", and "puzzles that lack a unique key move are often called cooked puzzles" (Grant, [part 3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsbfkMuaUxs)). In Portal 2D, routes may differ in which tile a shot is fired from, but a copy of the chamber with the key move made impossible (its wall face set to refuse portals) should come back unsolvable (candidate 11). This eases the tension above: a teach chamber needs one key move, not one route. **[covered in part: G38]**
- **Show it before requiring it.** Demonstrate a consequence on something unimportant first: "Letting players see other characters make mistakes that they'll need to avoid is an effective way to explain your puzzles" (Birdwell, [The Cabal](https://www.jmeiners.com/shamans/papers/art/half_life_design.pdf)); Q.U.B.E. 2 shows each mechanic working in a fixed position before the player can place it ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-the-i-qube-2-i-devs-built-a-better-massive-3d-puzzle-labyrinth)). In Portal 2D: a box the player doesn't need, lying where a door closes or a fizzler switches on, shows destruction and return before the player's own box is at stake; a mirror box already resting in a ray shows the turn. **[new]**
- **Make the player stop where it matters.** Testers rushed past what made the portals, so TC02 got "a mandatory pause in the action - what we call a gate" (Brashill, [commentary, TC02](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)). Turns rush no one, but a held key repeats and a slide carries the subject past, so where a chamber shows something the player must notice, make the route stop in front of it (a door to open, a button to press, a slide that ends facing it) rather than run past it. **[new]**
- **Correct the likely wrong belief.** Testers assumed orange was exit-only, so TC03 forces entering orange (commentary, TC03). Chamber 5 does this already for "the other end can be moved". **[covered in part: G42]**
- **Show that what looks lost isn't.** A Parabox tester "thought this mechanic put them into a lose state but it actually doesn't", so Traynor added "four levels that clarify this" (GDC 2024); his list makes it an idea source: "Clarify a misunderstanding or reinforce a theorem" ([cwpat.me](https://cwpat.me/misc/puzzle-level-idea-strategies/)). With no undo, a player who thinks they've lost presses Restart and learns a wrong rule. Portal 2D's likely false losses: a box breaking up (it flies home, G5), both portals fizzling (shoot again), a portal vanishing as a blocked wall shuts, a door closing behind a portal that still leads back. In cold playtests, note each restart and what came just before it; a restart right after one of these calls for a chamber that shows it can be undone (Part 5, idea 12). **[new]** (G15 covers the opposite: a real loss must look like one)
- **Teach the moves the controls hide.** Portal's testers didn't know they could place portals while falling, so TC15 got a puzzle where "the only way to solve it, was to use the mid-air placement tactic" ([commentary, TC15](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)). Portal 2D's hidden moves are verbs: turning in place with the turn key, the only way to face a free tile without stepping onto it and so to put a box down there (G8; the Clover corridor, idea 5, depends on it), and shooting through a door held open (idea 3). A line in the controls legend isn't a lesson: give each such move a chamber where it is the only way, before any chamber relies on it. **[covered in part: G40]** (G40 lists mechanics and elements, not moves)
- **Consistent cues build associations, and associations stick.** Portal's fling cue was learned "in much the same way that players learn to associate cubes with buttons" (commentary, TC10). Once early chambers always pair a button with a box, a button meant for the subject alone breaks the habit: good for a twist, confusing in a teach. **[new]**
- **Take the training wheels off at the end of a run.** Portal's last fling room removes the cue: "players must realize that they can double fling almost anywhere" (commentary, TC15). **[new]**
- **Re-teach after a big new mechanic.** Testers forgot flinging after the full gun arrived, so TC12 brings it back (commentary, TC12); Hazelden refreshes an old trick just before it's needed again ([YagmanX](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RR9-5FyKJOM)). **[new]**
- **Fix the sightline, not the text.** Portal 2's Fizzler Intro took four layouts. With two fizzlers and three areas "almost none of our playtesters could solve the puzzle"; an open space above one fizzler "didn't effectively convey" the shot; glass with several holes helped, since "the holes are there for a purpose", but players "could not figure out which side of the fizzler they needed to be on"; one hole at eye level, so that "shooting the portal from either side of the fizzler would be a valid solution", "tested really well" (Kohli, [commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)). Two lessons for Portal 2D: a narrowed opening says "use me" where an open wall says nothing (one gap in a fizzler run, one portal face among blocked walls); and when a choice doesn't matter to the idea (which side to shoot from, which portal first), make every option work, so no one stalls on it **[new]**. Make the rule visible when it fires: shots now flash on Portal 2's fizzlers. Portal 2D already does this with the fizzler splash, the light barrier reaction and the miss ripple. **[covered: game feature]**
- **Lead without seeming to lead.** Kim Swift's aim for Quantum Conundrum: "leading the player without making them feel like they're led" ([Game Developer 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-inclusive-design-of-kim-swift)). In Portal 2D: steer with the layout, not with text. Put the first thing to use in view of the start, and let sightlines and antlines point at the next step; in the cold playtest, check that testers describe the answer as something they worked out, not something they were shown. **[new]**
- **Show the wiring.** "Don't put a button on the ground and make the player stand on it to see what it does" (Grant, [30 lessons](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)); Portal 2 draws every connection, and the Puzzle Maker's dotted lines are automatic ([Gallant](https://gangles.ca/2012/05/13/perpetual-testing-initiative/)); Portal's lines "change color when powered up to clearly explain how the room is put together", since "the puzzle is not after all just figuring out how the level is rigged up" (Brown, [GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg)). **[covered: G33]**, but **[tension]**: G33 lets later chambers drop antlines, which turns "which button does what" into part of the puzzle. The Portal 2 community calls hidden key information "artificial difficulty" ([Idolon](https://thinking.withportals.com/forum/topic/portal-2-level-design-guidelines-t4291)). Drop an antline only where finding the link is meant to be the puzzle. Taylor's definition of noise explains why: "anything that occupies the player's working memory but it doesn't contribute to the puzzle" (GDC 2019). A link found without an antline must be remembered for the rest of the chamber; so must a box's start tile, which nothing on the floor marks once the box has moved (G6). Unless remembering is the puzzle, draw the antline, or let the player watch the box fly home before a route depends on it.
- **Don't drag.** Juul's testers of a casual game: "After 20 minutes, felt like I saw the whole game" ([Fear of Failing?](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). Mel skipped training and had to ship an easier mode ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portal_Stories:_Mel)). Both failures exist: one teach chamber per element, then move on.
- **Show levels.** Trivial levels that just show a consequence, which Parabox players found "delightful": "You don't always need a challenge" (Traynor, GDC 2024). **[new]** **[tension]**: players who expect a puzzle hunt for one. Where Portal 2's testers only had to portal into the next room, they "spent ages searching for a puzzle to solve in that first chamber", so Wheatley's lines became "remarkably insistent on the actual, seemingly trivial solution" ([Foster](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/p/level-with-me-adam-foster-part-2.html)). Portal 2D has no voice to insist, so a show chamber must look as simple as it is: few elements, the answer the first thing in view. A joke level (4.1, row 19) risks the same hunt.
- **Put the lesson next to its use.** Antichamber's fix for "I'm always lost" was to connect each teaching puzzle to where it's applied ([design3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WpNd6OqA4Hg)). **[covered: G43]**

### 3.4 Difficulty and pacing

**What makes a puzzle hard** (the research is consistent):

- _Not size, not length._ For 35 Sokoban levels, each with four boxes and of similar size and layout, the size of the state space didn't predict the median solving time (rank correlation ρ ≈ −0.07), and nor did other whole-graph measures. The shortest solution, counted in box pushes with walking left out, predicted it only weakly (0.47). Three measures did better: how many pushes on the shortest solution take the boxes farther, in total, from their targets (0.69); the fewest times any solution must switch from one box to another (0.74); and, best by rank, the fewest runs any solution needs with the boxes split into two groups (0.82). Two very similar levels took a median of 3 minutes (the larger, which splits into two rooms) and 54 (one compact room) ([Jarušek and Pelánek 2010](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/stairs2010-final.pdf)). The solver's fewest moves counts walking, which the study left out as adding nothing; the nearer match is the number of walk-then-one-action steps on the shortest route. For puzzles whose moves can be undone, like Rush Hour, length predicted difficulty well (ρ ≈ 0.90) ([2011](https://cdn.aaai.org/ocs/2518/2518-11200-1-PB.pdf)); Portal 2D sits between the two.
- _A simulated player._ The same study's second method walks the state graph at random but never into a dead state, favouring steps that bring it closer to the goal, more strongly as the goal nears, since "at the beginning humans explore the state space rather randomly, later … they move more straightforwardly to the goal". Its average step count matched the solving times best of all (Pearson 0.76, against decomposition's 0.63), though less well on order (Spearman 0.66). The solver could run it on any chamber it can search to the end: a backward pass from the completed states gives each state its distance to the exit (the "Dead ends" idea in `docs/solver.md`), then a few thousand weighted walks give a score. Portal 2D's hidden dead ends are where the paper's walk breaks, so let it enter a dead state now and then and charge it a restart: a chamber whose score jumps has costly dead ends. **[new]**
- _Bottlenecks._ Showing the same study's state graph of one Sokoban level, Grant points at "this really tight bottleneck": players spent most of their time before it and "hardly any time" after, and the level "takes an average of 30 to 40 minutes". "The bottlenecks are what create difficulty. And if you have multiple bottlenecks, that can make things even more difficult" ([part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)). The paper itself found players "finish the problem rather quickly" once they get "to one half of the distance between the start and goal state". One key move per chamber is plenty.
- _Flow, gem or slog._ Grant sorts puzzles by steps and difficulty: many easy steps is "a flow puzzle", "only one difficult step" is "a gem puzzle", "many steps in high difficulty" is "just a slog"; flows and gems "are usually the most suitable for video games" (part 2). Mark each chamber's card (3.1) as a gem (one key move, a short route around it) or a flow (several easy steps and no catch, such as a rest or a show level); a long route with more than one hard step is a slog: cut a step or split the chamber. **[new]**
- _One hard part, the rest turned down._ CC1 limited "its difficulty to one component throughout the vast majority of its levels", "dialing down other components to help the player focus"; designers spot difficulty that comes from the concept but "often underestimate" difficulty from the layout, such as length and a fixed order (J.B. Lewis, [In Defense of Consistency](https://forum.bitbusters.club/thread-81.html)). Name the one thing that makes a chamber hard and keep the rest plain: few kinds of element, a short route, sub-goals that can be done in any order. **[new]** (G40 keeps teach and test chambers to one mechanic, not to one source of difficulty)
- _Presentation alone._ The same Tower of Hanoi structure presented differently varied up to 16:1 in difficulty (Kotovsky, Hayes and Simon 1985, via secondary sources). Brown took "a good few minutes" over a Portal 2 puzzle he'd met almost exactly in The Turing Test, where "the light bridge is already over the button, you just have to remove it"; even Portal 2's version is "full of pretty obvious hints": the cube "starts off being in front of the laser", and "the only wall you can place a portal on" puts the bridge over the button ([GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg)). Portal 2D has the same dials: whether the player builds the setup or only triggers it (a mirror box already in the ray, 4.6 pattern 2), where boxes start (one starting in a ray shows that it stops rays), how many faces take portals, and whether the spot where the setup is made looks onto its target. Turn each towards hidden for a harder chamber, towards shown for a teach chamber. Start positions are a dial too: Portal 2's Triple Laser went from "permanently stuck" to fine with "incredibly subtle changes in the cubes' initial positions", and "We don't really know why" ([Foster](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/p/level-with-me-adam-foster-part-2.html)). When testers stall, try moving a box start a tile or two before redesigning, and recheck G5–G7, since a box's start is also where it lands when sent home. **[new]**
- _For laser-and-mirror puzzles specifically_, a study fitted player ratings of Lazors from features such as reflections in the solution and beam crossings; the counts of emitters and receivers barely mattered ([van Kreveld et al. 2015](https://ics-websites.science.uu.nl/docs/vakken/mscip/assignments/CIG2015-AutomatedPuzzleDifficultyEstimation.pdf)).
- _Designer heuristics_ (Square Enix Montréal, via [GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg)): the number of possible solutions ("the more there are the easier the puzzle is"), the number of steps ("more is more difficult but too many is tedious"), the number of options at each moment, and which mechanics must already be known, used to "put the puzzles in a sensible order". Every extra route the solver turns up makes a chamber easier, not just untidier. Too many options overwhelm; too few let players "just do the thing that's in front of them, over and over again, until they solve the puzzle" (Brown, Developing 9).

For Portal 2D: **make a chamber harder by tangling its subgoals, not by enlarging it** (one box needed on two buttons in turn; a portal placement for the first goal that breaks the second; a move that carries the box _away_ from its button first). **[new]** The solver's fewest moves and fewest portals are only part of the picture; the study's best predictor by rank can be measured the way it was there. Label each step that changes the chamber by the thing it moves (each box, the blue portal, the orange portal), split those things into two groups, and find the route that takes up a group the fewest times; the score is that count for the best split. The paper's easy example scores 2 (one half, then the other) and the hard one 7. It's a cheapest-first search over pairs of state and group last used, where a switch costs 1 and any other step 0, which the solver's cost queue already supports. Treating each portal as a unit is this document's translation. **[new]**

**The designer can't judge it.** Teikari: "very easy for that designer to become blind to a lot of the difficulty" ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/when-we-made-baba-is-you/)); Brown: the creator "just cannot gauge their quality or difficulty"; Snakebird researchers found designers misjudged difficulty even with solver numbers in front of them ([Guzdial et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.04147)). **[covered: G39, G43]**

**Pacing shape: a sawtooth, not a ramp.** Linehan and colleagues coded Let's Plays of Portal, Portal 2, Braid and Lemmings: each skill is introduced alone in a simple puzzle, practised and combined, and complexity rises until the next skill resets it ([CHI PLAY 2014](https://cora.ucc.ie/handle/10468/3461)). Talos 1's steady ramp made its last third "mentally taxing … discouraging", so Talos 2 used "many smaller ramps" ([Thinky Games](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B3yoVxhld4Q)). Baba's areas each "reset the difficulty curve at least a little bit" ([GDC Plays](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RCk0bipJx84)). **[covered: G40, G41]**

**Rests.** Portal's testers tired unless "the more complicated, deliberately paced puzzles" were broken up "with problems that required the player to perform a much simpler task under time pressure" (Rickey, [commentary, escape](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)); Portal 2's were "fatigued at solving so many complex test chambers in a row", so an elevator ride became "a short break" (Egan, [commentary, _Elevator Fatigue_](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)). Taylor found that levels "after a bunch of hard ones where it's just a big open space just to let people exhale" were "really important", and counts a "palette cleanser" among a level's purposes. Portal 2D has no time pressure (fact 3), so its rest is a short, easy chamber between hard ones, such as a show level (3.3); it can be easy without being big (G36). **[new]**

**Puzzles alone go dry.** Portal's playtests found that "fifteen to thirty minutes into the game, the experience started to get a little dry"; GLaDOS's announcements gave testers "the incentive to keep playing" (Barnett, Swift and Wolpaw, [Game Developer 2008](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/thinking-with-portals-creating-valve-s-new-ip)). Portal 2D has no narrator, so its chambers carry this alone: in full-run playtests, note when testers start to flag, and have something new arrive around then, a new element or a show level. **[new]**

**The finale needn't be the hardest.** "The more complex, the longer players would take, resulting in bad pacing"; Portal's most climactic moment was "about the easiest puzzle in Portal" ([GDC 2008 slides](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2008/GDC2008_PortalPostMortem.pdf)); Cosmic Express's required finale is its most resented level ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/all-roads/)). Portal had time pressure for drama; Portal 2D doesn't, so its last chamber needs synthesis and spectacle, not the hardest catch. **[new]**

**Optional peaks and parallel options.** Almost every game researched keeps its hardest puzzles optional (Talos 2's lost puzzles and Golden Gates, Lara Croft GO's post-story chapter, Snowman's optional levels, Baba's extras) and offers several open puzzles at once, because "it's very easy to get mentally stuck" (Teikari). Talos 1's open structure was itself a test result: "Getting stuck on puzzles in testing led them to The Talos Principle's nonlinear structure, so that players always have multiple puzzles to tackle" ([PC Gamer on Croteam's GDC 2015 talk](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-talos-principle-started-as-serious-sam-4/), the reporter's words). Portal 2D's chambers are a numbered list, but none is locked: a stuck player can already open the next. **[tension]**: Valve's co-op hub, which let players pick any chamber, "prevented us from doing sufficient training", so it went linear ("By going linear, we could guarantee prior knowledge", commentary). With 20 chambers and no optional layer, the closest equivalents are (a) the par block for fewest moves and portals, which Valve itself moved out of the story into optional Challenge maps: "We tried to fit that concept into the story mode, but were never quite able to sell it" (commentary, TC13), and (b) a late chamber that is hard and nothing after it depends on. Blow names a use for (a): first make sure "everybody will understand everything that I want them to", then "make some things subtle again" for players who are "extra attentive" (IndieCade 2011). In a required list, a subtle truth must not gate the next chamber; the fewest-moves or fewest-portals route, or a second route in a twist or combine chamber, can hold it.

**Ordering is a skill of its own.** Traynor's verbs: insert, modify, delete, reorder, make optional. When testers stalled at C after A and B, he inserted a bridging puzzle; when one still stalled, C became optional ([GDC 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HAvS-RwkjdA)). His list gives the method for the bridge, "Bisect two levels": "design the puzzle that would be 'between' those two" ([cwpat.me](https://cwpat.me/misc/puzzle-level-idea-strategies/)). Portal did this from its first playtests: "If more than one playtester had problems in a certain area we would break out problem-solving concepts into separate sections". Its first energy-ball room needed the ball redirected through a portal twice, and testers "would get stuck in this section for a very long time"; two rooms needing one redirect each went in before it, and players then "managed to grasp the concept much quicker" (Barnett, Swift and Wolpaw, [Game Developer 2008](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/thinking-with-portals-creating-valve-s-new-ip); the commentary's TC08 node tells the same story: "we inserted two test chambers before this one"). In Portal 2D, a chamber whose ray needs two turns (a mirror box and a portal pair) comes after chambers that each need one. Foster calls this Valve's usual route: "not to change it directly, but modify previous puzzles so that they vaguely hint at particular mechanics" ([Radiator Blog](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/p/level-with-me-adam-foster-part-2.html)); _vaguely_ matters, since an earlier chamber that rehearses the answer robs the hard one of its aha (3.5, "Plant fragments"). Croteam had ~20 people play every greyboxed puzzle, rate difficulty and fun, time them and sort ([Nordic Game 2016](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D0ErxdacTT8)); in the written report of their GDC 2015 talk, the designers "extensively tested each other's puzzles and rated them for fun and difficulty", and outside testing then "cut out redundant puzzles" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-talos-principle-started-as-serious-sam-4/)). With one designer, the cold playtest is the outside stage; when two chambers' cards (3.1) say the same thing, cut or merge one. Ranking by player data takes far more players than Portal 2D has: the Sokoban study used the median time of solved attempts, shuffled the order for each player, and still needed 2,071 solves by 294 players for 35 levels ([Jarušek and Pelánek 2010](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/stairs2010-final.pdf)). A handful of testers shows where a chamber stalls, not how it ranks. **[covered in part: G43]**; the bridging-chamber move is **[new]**.

### 3.5 Creating "aha" moments instead of trial and error

- **Assumption → catch → revelation** (Mark Brown, [What Makes a Good Puzzle?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsjC6fa_YBg), informed by Hazelden and Square Enix Montréal):
  - The goal must be obvious: "The player shouldn't be figuring out what to do - just how to do it." In Portal 2D, show the exit and what holds it shut from the start.
  - The catch is "a logical contradiction, where two things are seemingly in direct conflict"; the minimal case is a button that opens a door you can't reach while standing on it.
  - It resolves by changing the order, starting from a different position, or (the "gold standard") a revelation: a "non-obvious - but also totally logical consequence of the game's rules" that joins the player's toolbox.
  - The best puzzles lure the player into a wrong assumption that walks them into the catch. That gives a starting point instead of overwhelm, builds a model of the puzzle while chasing the wrong idea, all but guarantees a first failure (so the player feels stumped instead of stumbling into the answer), and focuses them on the catch. Weak puzzles have a catch too easy to resolve, no assumption (so players stumble into it), or too little revelation (so it feels like busywork). **[new]**
  - Make the lure fail plainly. Rise of the Tomb Raider places a dropping platform "significantly far away so it's clearly impossible to get there in time", so "the player immediately knows to break the assumption and try a different approach" (Brown). Portal 2D has no timing, but a near miss still reads as nearly right: a slide stopping one tile short of the button, a ray ending one tile from the receiver, the only portal face one row off the line. Unless the near miss is the clue, make the lure fail by a clear margin or for a reason the game shows at once (a door shutting on the step off its button, a band flaring on a refused shot). **[new]**
  - And soon. **[tension]** on how long the wrong path may run: J.B. Lewis warns that a "making-the-player-realize-that-he-or-she-had-been-doing-everything-incorrectly-for-20-minutes moment" will "probably … frustrate more people instead", most of all "where failure involves returning all the way to the very beginning" ([The Simple Things](https://forum.bitbusters.club/thread-91.html)). With no undo, show the wrong assumption wrong within a few moves, before the player has built much on it (candidate 7; the near-miss census in 3.7).
- **Make the catch provable.** Grant's "aporia" is believing a puzzle is impossible ("You may even feel like you have a proof that it's impossible") while knowing it has an answer; it "promises you that a powerful Eureka moment is coming", and "this state of aporia is highly motivating" ([part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)). It needs a small state: "statefulness can make it harder to understand that this eureka moment was necessary … because it just seems like there are so many things to try" ([part 3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zsbfkMuaUxs)). Where a Portal 2D chamber's catch must be felt, keep the movable parts few (one box, few faces that take portals), so the player can rule out the obvious routes by reasoning. **[new]**
- **Why it works (psychology).** Insight problems stall at an impasse until the solver drops a constraint they imposed on themselves; dropping a broad, deeply trained belief is harder than a narrow, local one ([Knoblich et al. 1999](https://www.kognition.uni-koeln.de/literature/Knoblich_etal_1999.pdf)). So a twist that breaks something earlier chambers trained hard is a harder aha than one that breaks a local assumption. Moves that seem to go away from the goal are among the strongest difficulty predictors (Jarušek and Pelánek). **[new]**
- **Truth, not hardness.** "You don't make good puzzles by trying to make things that are hard"; an aha that doesn't show something true about the rules "is going to feel false and empty" (Blow, [IndieCade 2011](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OGSeLSmOALU)). Grant separates eureka (the pleasure of understanding, which survives being shown the answer) from fiero (pride in beating a challenge) and warns against designing for fiero ([part 1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oCHciE9CYfA)). **[new]**
- **Let the rules set the puzzle, not the designer.** Removing everything arbitrary is "abdicating authorship over the puzzle": the player then reads "mother nature", not "the mind of the designer", which "makes the puzzles feel more fair" (ten Bosch, IndieCade 2011). Portal 2D's rules are pinned, so the risk is in the chamber: a draft that needs a pile of one-off patches (3.1, step 3), such as a lone blocked wall tile on the exact face a shortcut uses, reads as the designer saying no. Prefer a fix the room's own shape explains, and when patches pile up, rethink the room. **[new]**
- **Trick versus revelation.** A Braid puzzle that works at only one moment "felt more like a trick than a revelation", partly because players couldn't experiment toward it (Brown). Snakebird's level 11, whose trick is only "eat the cherry on the first loop", "teaches little" ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/?p=13654)). No one-off tricks: a method used once leaves players paranoid on every later puzzle (Grant). **[new]**
- **Kill trial and error.** "If the solution can only be reached by trial and error or plain luck, it's a bad puzzle" ([Gilbert 1989](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)). Keep the steps few but "the chance of any … particular attempt at solution being the right one" low: Miegakure's temple block comes out "just by three steps", but "those three steps need to be really understood in order to be done", since the block can be in so many places (ten Bosch, IndieCade 2011). In Portal 2D the dial is how many faces take portals: a route of two shots and a carry is quick to play but hard to stumble into when many faces take portals, while a room with one portal face teaches and doesn't test. Gate so that stumbling dead-ends (Valve TC01). Taylor's red flags in playtests: "was that right?", "oh, I solved it", and a player "attempting an impossible action repeatedly … because it's not obviously impossible". Portal 2D has no execution skill, so the repeated tries are refusals: shooting the wall behind an end plate (G12), shooting across a light barrier as if it were a laser, shooting while carrying, putting a box down while on a switch (G19). Each already shows a miss ripple or plays the error sound; a tester who keeps repeating one means the layout makes it look possible, so move the elements apart or give the shot another wall. Always-on rays invite wiggling: an accidental solve "can rob the player of understanding" ([Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/reflections-on-a-design/)), so put the insight where wiggling can't reach it, such as which wall must hold a portal or where a box must already be before a slide. **[new]**
- **Plant fragments; don't hand over the idea.** A puzzle right before Portal 2's _Bonk Heads_, "where two cubes repeatedly collide", cut solve time but "almost completely robbed the appeal"; fragments planted four maps earlier let players "own that exciting dual collision epiphany" (commentary). Wood: teach a thing and "you want them to forget about it just long enough" ([Kiwi Talkz](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3dYAZxr2FbY)). For _Bonk Heads_, Valve planted three shards: the part move "four chambers earlier", a repeated side action to "subconsciously prime the thought of midair collision", and a room laid out so players "would see the entire space as a symmetrical whole" (Barnett, commentary). **[new]**
- **Prime a forgotten old tool just before.** Portal 2's testers fixated on a new excursion funnel, "forgetting that they had a portal gun", so Valve put the same portal move just before the chamber, where players met "the exact same scenario, but in a different context" (Cliffe, [commentary, _Misleading Fixations_](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)). This doesn't contradict _Bonk Heads_: priming an old tool the chamber needs is fine; handing over its catch isn't. In Portal 2D the likely fixation is an animated element (a light barrier, a ray, a fizzler's field, 3.8): when the answer is a plain crossing, make the chamber's first step, or the chamber before it, need the same crossing in a plain room. **[new]**
- **Same pieces, new consequence.** Stephen's Sausage Roll "never really introduces new objects. It's just teaching you about new consequences of things that were already there" (Hazelden to Lavelle, [podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4P2iC7YQu1k)). This is your twist chamber's job description. **[covered: G40 twist]**
- **Difficulty from what's missing.** Baba's level 7 drops a rule nearly every earlier level had; players take a long time to notice ([Gamelab](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8eTFea_UZcc)). **[new]**

### 3.6 Classic patterns and tropes

The full catalog, each pattern mapped to Portal 2D's elements, is in Part 4. The ones every source names: the catch (two goals in conflict), the lure (an easy earlier use primes a wrong move), both orders fail (interleave), the single key move, the counterintuitive move, the hidden consequence, prepare then trigger, a proxy for being in two places, the reprise (same layout, one change), the sequence that breaks its own pattern, and the alternate solution turned into its own level.

### 3.7 Testing and validation

**Playtesting.**

- _Watch in silence; trust behaviour._ Valve's Half-Life testers played for two hours while designers sat behind them, "not allowed to say anything"; a session gave about 100 action items ([Birdwell](https://www.jmeiners.com/shamans/papers/art/half_life_design.pdf)). "Game designs are hypotheses / Playtests are experiments"; think-aloud is "Effective for 'why' questions" and brings up "unnoticed details", but can "Interfere with gameplay" and is "Inaccurate and biased", while asked afterwards, in a Q&A, "People don't know why they do what they do" ([Ambinder, GDC 2009](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/GDC2009_ValvesApproachToPlaytesting.pdf)): catch the why while it happens, and treat an explanation given after the session as a guess. Watching also skews: "Presence of observers can bias results" and a "Salient event can slant interpretation" (same), so write each stall down with its time as it happens, and judge the session from the list, not from its most memorable moment. Read body language, not what testers say (Swift, [IGS 2007](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2F0SVZA9fIo)); watching "gives you much more information than simply having them fill in a questionnaire" (Barnett, Swift and Wolpaw, [Game Developer 2008](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/thinking-with-portals-creating-valve-s-new-ip)). **[covered in part: G39]**
- _When, and from where._ Narbacular Drop's team playtested only in its last month, when the problems were "far too late to actually fix"; on Portal they tested a rough first room in the first week and brought in "someone that hadn't played the game before" every week, watching each "from beginning to end" (same). In Portal 2D: put a draft chamber in front of a fresh player as soon as it can be played through, before building the chambers that lean on it, and start the tester at least at the teach chamber of its run, since a chamber's difficulty depends on what came before it. **[new]** (G39 says who, not when) **[tension]** with rationing first-time players under _How many_: with a few friends, spend early fresh eyes where a wrong lesson would spread, such as a run's teach chamber.
- _A session script_ (from Schell-based course slides, [PDF](https://stewart.sdsu.edu/cs583_spr2013/LearningXNA4_lects/JesseSchell_playtesting101.pdf)): go in with specific questions; tell testers you're testing the game, not them; say you won't help; "You should be writing down about one new problem per minute"; watch the face more than the screen; "Don't ask your playtesters to be game designers"; "Don't defend your game". Valve also ignores the fixes testers suggest ([GMTK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9Yomqk0C6kE)). **[new]**
- _Ask alone first, together last._ Questions after play suffer from "Group biases (anchoring, social pressure, saliency, etc.)", so Valve's order is "Survey / Individual Q&A / Group Q&A" (Ambinder). Friends compare notes, so right after playing each fills a short fixed form alone (each chamber's challenge from 1 to 7, the chambers ranked by how much they liked them, and whether they've played Portal or other puzzle games), then talks with you one to one, and only then with the others. Ask what happened ("where did you stop?"), not whether something worked. A fixed form makes rounds comparable, but decide changes from what testers did, not from the numbers. **[new]**
- _Record._ Traynor used screen recordings with testers thinking aloud, fresh non-expert testers each time, batches of one or two, and watched each video through, taking notes, "before making changes as opposed to pausing the video every time I wanted to make a change"; he timed the parts of a puzzle on the video and kept testing "more or less constant in the background … as opposed to like backloading it"; about 15 full-game tests, and afterwards he wished he'd done more ([GDC 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HAvS-RwkjdA)). He treated "small bumps" as potentially "a big hurdle for another player"; with about five friends a round, one tester's bump may be the only warning. A reorder renumbers chambers (G43), so label each recording with the chamber's permanent id and the commit, not its number. Croteam asked testers "why did you do that when you could have done something else". **[new]** His full-game testers are a **[tension]** with rationing (_How many_): a full run covers the late chambers, but spends every chamber's first look at once.
- _How many._ Five testers per round find most usability problems; three rounds of five beat one round of fifteen, since fixes need retesting ([Nielsen](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/); usability research, but the iteration argument transfers). A person is a first-time player of a chamber only once ("tissue testers"), so ration them: stagger which friend sees which chamber first, and keep one or two untested for the final order ([Eastshade](https://eastshade.com/2015/10); Puzzledorf). **[new]**
- _Read the analytics as the playtest nobody watches._ For Valve, "Playtesting continues after we ship" through "Gameplay stats": deaths, level times and the share of players who finished each campaign (Ambinder). Portal 2D's analytics events are that record: with no undo, a restart is its death, and quits and abandons are where a friend gave up; each event's time, moves and portals can be set against the recorded solution's. Ambinder's cautions say how to read them: "Averages hide extreme examples", "Miss nuance (lacking context)", "Can see 'illusory' patterns". So read each friend's row per chamber, not an average, and treat a chamber whose restarts or quits stand out as the place to watch the next fresh tester, not as a verdict. The events carry no tile, so they can't show where in a chamber players stall. **[covered in part: G43]**
- _Let a playtest settle a tension._ Playtests "Solve design arguments" through experiments that "Compare two or more conditions" (Ambinder). Where this document marks a **[tension]** that matters for a chamber (antlines kept or dropped, one route early or several), build both versions and give each to different fresh friends. Two or three players per version show only large differences, and it uses up fresh testers, so keep it for a choice the campaign repeats. **[new]**
- _Ask what each element does._ Portal 2's testers walked through a fizzler "in almost every map", yet thought one map was their first and "weren't able to report the grid's properties"; Valve changed the field until testers understood it (Grimes, [commentary, _Emancipation Grid_](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)). After a cold playtest, ask the tester what each element in the chamber did: one they walked past but can't describe hasn't been taught, whichever chamber it first appeared in. **[covered in part: G39]**
- _When it's done._ "Puzzle design could be considered 'done' when most playtesters would find the puzzles fun, and make it through without getting irretrievably stuck"; taking a while is fine, but an hour on "the same, unsuccessful thing over and over again" is not, and "Getting the puzzle immediately is bad, but it still has to be solveable" ([Foster](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/p/level-with-me-adam-foster-part-2.html)). In Portal 2D, a tester who restarts to replay the same route is stuck on one idea, the rabbit trail of 3.8. **[covered in part: G39]**
- _Who._ Testers unscreened for puzzle experience made Q.U.B.E. too easy for its fans: "The important thing is you need to get the right players" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-the-i-qube-2-i-devs-built-a-better-massive-3d-puzzle-labyrinth)). Valve mixed "total Portal newbies" and "Portal pros" weekly ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/interview-valve-on-their-insane-portal-2-ideas/)).
- _Stance._ "It's not the player's fault -- it's your fault" (Swift, [GDC China 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/10-years-of-design-lessons-from-em-portal-em-s-kim-swift)). **[tension]** on what to do with negative feedback: Croteam cut a cluster that split testers half and half ([Thinky Games](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KbMIp3q2Tc0)); Blow warns that cutting "anything that a percentage of people find hard" leaves "this nucleus in the middle", and keeps what a quarter of players hate ([noclip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YdSdvIRkkDY)). With no optional chambers, Croteam's stance fits Portal 2D's required list better.
- _Retest after every change._ A retextured door that silently stopped opening broke Talos until a bot reached it ([Nordic Game 2016](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D0ErxdacTT8)); its puzzles were "susceptible to accidental errors made while tuning or decorating levels, which broke it in such a way that you couldn't finish the game" ([Ladavac, GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/the-talos-principle-underwent-15000-hours-of-playtesting-but-not-by-humans-interview/)); a texture changed behind a ladder broke a Left 4 Dead session ([Faliszek](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/valve-s-faliszek-playtesters-aren-t-idiots-it-s-you)). In Portal 2D the art can't change the rules, but a layout edit that looks harmless can: a wall placed beside a fizzler turns its connectors and changes which walls take portals (G12). **[covered: G12, G37, G38, replay check]**

**Machine checks.** No source shows Valve using a solver for Portal or Portal 2; validation was human. Portal 2D's recorded solutions, replay check and solver already go beyond them, and match the best practice found: Stephen's Sausage Roll had "one button that can just play through the whole game"; A Monster's Expedition stores the intended solution of every island as a replay and turns bugs into replay test cases ([ThinkyCon 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=98tmqN0bUGc)). **[covered: G37, G38]** What the research adds:

- _Prove the negative._ A designer proves a puzzle _has_ a good solution; a check must prove _no_ solution is a shortcut, which takes exhaustive search ([Smith et al.](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/smith2013qop.pdf)). A tester beating the designer's best by two moves made one developer write a solver ([Wallerstein](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/just-a-level-solver-why-my-simple-puzzle-game-took-me-over-a-year-to-complete)). **[covered: G38]**
- _Concept-required check._ Tag each chamber with the moves it introduces, requires and wants players to try and fail; check that required moves appear in every solution ([Vet and van Rozen 2024](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/34332/34332.pdf)). G38's "remove the links to an element, expect unsolvable" is one case; the general form also covers moves ("solvable with zero portals?", "solvable without picking up the box?"). **[new]**
- _Near-miss census._ List the dead states within one or two actions of the intended route: those are the mistakes players will actually make. Each should be visibly fatal or deliberately instructive (synthesis of Vet and van Rozen's near-solution failure traces and Grant's "negative confirmers"). Ten Bosch gives the reason: each attempt "reveals something different", and "the space around the truth that you were trying to point out is also often … even more valuable than the truth itself" (IndieCade 2011). So judge a draft by what its likely wrong tries show: a wrong portal pair, a box on the wrong button or a slide that overshoots should end where the player can see what stopped it (a miss ripple, a band reacting, a door staying shut, an antline left unlit). **[new]**
- _One-change sweeps._ One added object can swing a solution a lot: one spike in a Snakebird level took the shortest solution from 28 to 41 moves (search snippets of [Sturtevant et al. 2020](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/7421)); in Fling!, adding or removing one ball flipped a level between solvable and unsolvable ([Cook](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-saturday-paper-exhausting)). For a draft, script the solver over single changes (one wall face set to refuse portals, one wall tile added or removed, a box start moved one tile) and list which changes lengthen the route or remove an unintended shorter one. Keep the human as the judge. **[new]**
- _Forbidden facts._ Monster's Expedition's editor shows "X" markers where land must never go "because it would cause unintended solutions". A per-chamber list of "must never be possible" facts (this face never takes a portal; this tile is never reached before the box is placed) could be checked by the solver. **[new]**
- _Draw the graph._ For slides, every tile a slide can stop on is a node and every slide an edge; for links, the reachable state graph (Johansen's [PuzzleGraph](https://blog.runevision.com/2016/04/puzzlegraph-puzzle-state-space.html): "every change in state should be significant and correspond to a choice made by the player"). Both expose dead ends and false choices a layout sketch hides. **[covered in part: G20, G24]**

### 3.8 Player psychology

- **Players should blame themselves.** "The players should always blame themselves for failure": warn of the danger and show a way out, and they try harder; fail them without warning and they blame the game (Birdwell, [The Cabal](https://www.jmeiners.com/shamans/papers/art/half_life_design.pdf)). Players who blamed "the game was too hard" rated it lower ([Juul](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). **[new]**
- **Stress blocks learning.** "Players Learn Better When Not Stressed" ([GDC 2008 slides](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2008/GDC2008_PortalPostMortem.pdf)); frustration triggers fight-or-flight and inhibits problem solving, and stuck players fall into a "cognitive rut" that a break can fix ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2017/11/22/why-you-get-stuck-in-games-and-what-you-can-do-about-it)). In a turn-based game the stress is complexity: teach in a calm, small chamber before a combine chamber needs it. Portal's first double-fling room also held an energy-ball puzzle, "too much for most playtesters. Overwhelmed players tend not to digest new information", so it was cut to the double fling alone (Swift, [commentary, TC15](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)). In Portal 2D, the first chamber that needs a new move holds no other new or hard element: the first mirror-box chamber isn't also the first light-barrier chamber. **[covered: G40 teach]**
- **Trust is a resource.** Decoys cost it (Puzzledorf), ambiguous solutions cost it (Taylor), one-off tricks and inconsistent rules cost it (Grant), and a secret found without its clue makes players search every corner from then on ("poisoning the well", Bob Bates via Grant). Players push through "this seems impossible" only for a designer they trust (Grant's "aporia"). **[new]** Grant calls the conventions a designer keeps to play fair "square dealing", such as escape rooms' "one key, one lock", and "in video games especially it's also important to communicate those rules to the player" (part 2). For Portal 2D, write down the campaign's conventions and break none by accident: for example, whether every box in a chamber is needed (a spare box, 4.3 row 10, breaks that), and whether every link has an antline until a chamber plainly stops drawing them.
- **Everything visible reads as meaningful.** "The perceivable part of an affordance is a signifier", and accidental cues are read like deliberate ones ([Norman](https://jnd.org/signifiers-not-affordances/)); a "weird awkward niche" is "probably there for a reason" (Sean Barrett, [Electron Dance](https://www.electrondance.com/all-roads/)); Valve made decorative catwalks reachable because testers kept trying to reach them (commentary, Escape). Players also assume objects stay where they're useful: playing Robert Yang's Puzzle Maker level, built so that "the solution should involve undoing progress toward the solution", Foster found the cube powering a tractor beam "too strongly locked in position. It took me far too long to realise I had to carry it past the piston", and suggested "just two cubes total"; Yang: "You always assume you have exactly what you need" ([Radiator Blog](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/p/level-with-me-adam-foster-part-2.html)). In Portal 2D a box on a button whose door is open reads as done, so a chamber whose answer lifts it off again (4.1, row 5) should have no spare box to try first (4.1, row 11). On a single-screen grid with no decoration, every tile shape is read as intent. **[new]**
- **Symmetry makes every break in it read as meant.** Symmetry gives a layout "much more of a sense of being 'designed', with otherwise arbitrary shapes and details taking on a new importance" (Foster, on level architecture rather than puzzles); Taylor streamlined Limelight's levels towards symmetry, which is "easier to visually parse" (GDC 2019). On one screen, a mirrored chamber points at its one odd tile: put the break where the answer is, and check that a wall added only to stop a shortcut (3.1, step 3) isn't the loudest odd tile in the room. **[new]**
- **Match what matters with what stands out.** "Things that are important should stick out. Things that stick out should be important" (Grant, [part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUi2vMZajco)). Eyes go to movement and light ([Unity](https://unity.com/blog/level-design-basics)). In Portal 2D, lasers, open portals and rays glow, fizzler fields drift and light barriers flicker, so an animated element is noticed before a static one: a moving distractor pulls harder than a still one. **[new]**
- **Fair and unfair red herrings.**
  - _Unfair:_ decoration salient enough to seem pertinent and leftovers of removed puzzles (Grant's "dressings" and "ghost puzzles"); a "rabbit trail", a wrong approach that can't be proven wrong, which is "the most demotivating, especially for novices" (Grant). "I never design with red herrings. The players will create their own" (Eric Harshbarger via [Room Escape Artist](https://roomescapeartist.com/2019/02/10/red-herrings/)).
  - _Fair:_ a lure built from real rules that the player's own reasoning produces (Brown's assumption), or several plausible uses of the same real elements, only one of which works. An affordance you never need is Grant's "distractor", which he files apart from the misleading kinds: "It's not a red herring that's going to confuse you" (part 2). An unused stretch of portal wall is fine; "a singled out portable wall between black walls that looks like it HAS to be used" isn't ([ThinkingWithPortals](https://thinking.withportals.com/forum/topic/puzzle-producing-process-t5983)).
  - _Fix:_ a wrong approach that "immediately leads to a negative confirmer" is a fair "deadend approach"; when testers spend long on one that doesn't, "really sealing off rabbit trails is a good idea": "either prevent them from trying it or add a negative confirmer of some kind" (Grant, part 2). With no undo, a wrong approach in Portal 2D should fail visibly within a few moves (a missed shot ripples, a fizzled box flies home, a door stays shut); otherwise close it off, for instance with a face that refuses portals. **[covered in part: G15]**
  - _Test:_ whether it regularly misleads testers, whatever the intent (Spira). **[new]**
- **Confirm progress.** "Realizing that you're on the right track is one of the most dopaminergic things" (Grant). In Portal 2D, an antline turning amber, a door opening or a receiver lighting is a confirmer; a first sure sub-goal within the first few moves hooks the player (Gilbert: "Letting the player know at least the first sub-goal is essential"). **[new]**
- **Separate independent sub-puzzles visibly.** Portal 2 co-op players who could wander between areas "logically assumed that the individual puzzles were part of one big puzzle" (commentary, airlocks). **[new]**
- **Let a solved part read as done.** An extra Portal 2 puzzle hidden beyond the reflector cube "took players so long to get to it, that they were confused about which elements were still relevant", so the space was condensed and the exit put near the start ([commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal_2)). Portal 2D keeps the whole chamber on screen, so a finished part stays in view: keep the chamber compact, and let what is done look done (its door open, its antline lit) so the player can tell which elements still matter. **[new]**
- **Hints.** Portal 2D has none, and the evidence doesn't push for them: in Refraction (50,000 students) every hint design tested lowered performance ([O'Rourke et al. 2014](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/orourke2014hsm.pdf); children in an educational game, so transfer is unproven). Talos's own hint system was "too hard to get any hints at all" (Ladavac). Clearer chambers and earlier fragments beat hints. **[new]** **[tension]**: Valve broke its own rule twice in Portal. Momentum, "the hardest concept to convey", went "through more design iterations than virtually any other part of the game", and in the end "We even have the AI voice pretty explicitly explain the elements of the puzzle, something we avoided throughout most of the rest of the game" ([commentary, TC10](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)); TC17's testers left the box behind until it was fixed "by having the AI talk about the box. A lot." (TC17). Both times the words said what something is or does, not how to solve the puzzle, and came after layout fixes ran out: what `docs/hints.md` designs for Portal 2D's first chambers. The evidence against hints toward a solution still stands.

## Part 4 — Pattern catalog, mapped to Portal 2D

Patterns from the games and the generic writing, grouped by the Portal 2D element they rest on. Each row names the source, says how it's built with Portal 2D's elements, and adds a check against your rules where one applies. Part 3 links most of the sources; the mechanic-specific writing (ice puzzles, one-way tiles, laser mazes, switch logic) is summarised at the end of each section. Take one pattern per chamber: stacking patterns you admire is how J.B. Lewis's _Think Tank_ drifted from slot 39 to 133 (3.1, the card).

**Five facts about Portal 2D that change how outside patterns translate:**

1. **No AND gate.** Two buttons or receivers on one element close it when either is released or darkens (guideline 4). Portal 2's "several inputs on one door" becomes doors in series, or a receiver that switches on a second emitter whose ray reaches a second receiver.
2. **Links only open things when activated.** Pressing, using or lighting unblocks; releasing or darkening blocks again. "Press X to close Y" can't be built directly. Exclusive states have to come from layout: one box for two buttons, a mirror box steering a ray between two receivers, a turnable arrow, a switch that sends a box home (which releases its button).
3. **No hidden state, no timing, no momentum, no undo.** Every pattern that relies on fake walls, a clock, speed or an undo cushion is out. Slides are the only "keep moving" rule, and they resolve within one step.
4. **Carrying, not pushing.** Push-block deadlocks don't exist; a box is also a wall while it rests; full hands mean no portals.
5. **Shots pass open doors, lasers, ditches, boxes and arrows.** Most portal shortcuts come from this (guideline 14).

## Part 5 — Chamber ideas, by element and part

Prose sketches, not layouts: none has been built, solved or tested. Each names its part (teach, test, twist, combine, as in guideline 40), the pattern it comes from, and the guidelines to check when building it. They're grouped by element and slotted into neither plan.

What already exists, so the ideas avoid repeating it: chambers 4 and 5 teach and test slide floors, 6 and 7 teach and twist turnable arrows, 8 and 9 teach and test rays through portals, and the 04b file lists six ideas left by stage 04a (a fizzler mid-slide, a box carried across slides, an arrow held by a box, arrows changing where a slide ends, a ray crossing the tile before its own door, a mirror box carried around a fizzler). Gaps worth noting: **no chamber uses a light barrier or a mirror box yet**, and only chambers 1 and 3 use fizzlers.

### 5.1 Portals (basics)

1. **Gated islands** (teach or test). Three or four floor islands in a row, separated by lasers or open ditches, each with exactly one wall face that takes portals and sees the next island. The only way forward is a crossing per island, in order; a wrong shot is fixed by re-shooting. _From:_ Portal TC01's gating. _Teaches:_ aiming from the right tile, and that either portal can be the entrance. _Check:_ guideline 17 (each island has a face it can shoot from to come back), guideline 12 (no island's face is the wall at the end of a laser run).
2. **Shoot past what you can't walk past** (laser teach). A corridor to the exit cut by a run of lasers; one wall beyond the run takes portals, one wall on the subject's side does too. _From:_ Portal: The Flash Version's blue plasma wall. _Teaches:_ lasers block walking, not shots. _Check:_ the wall beyond must not be the end-plate wall of the laser run (guideline 12).
3. **Hold the door with a shot** (door teach, or a twist after box-and-button chambers). A button opens a door, and there is no box. Standing on the button, the subject faces the open doorway and a wall beyond it that takes portals; it shoots, steps off (the door closes), shoots the second portal on its own side and crosses. _From:_ Portal's TC11, which made a lesson of the move TC04 blocked as a cheat; also Flash Task 20. Valve's testers stalled twice: with a floor button they got "stuck searching hopelessly for a box", and with a pedestal button they "still had trouble realizing they needed to shoot a portal through the door", until a ticking sound cued them to act while the door was open ([commentary, TC11](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Portal)). Portal 2D can use neither fix (a switch opens a door for good, and nothing is timed), so the cue must come from the layout: no box anywhere in the chamber, the button tile looking straight through the doorway at the wall that takes portals, an antline from the button to the door. Expect cold testers to hunt for a box. _Teaches:_ open doors pass shots; a door closing doesn't close portals. _Check:_ the far wall must not be the tile right behind the door (those faces never take portals); after this chamber, every box-and-door chamber must decide whether this move is allowed (Part 3.2).
4. **The missing rule** (light barrier teach by contrast, or laser twist). Two routes to the exit area: one cut by a light barrier run, one by a laser run, each with a wall beyond that takes portals. Shooting across the light barrier misses (the band reacts); across the laser it works. _From:_ Baba's level 7 ("difficulty from what is missing"). _Teaches:_ light barriers stop shots, lasers don't. _Check:_ the light barrier's own end-plate walls take no portals (guideline 12); give the light barrier no link, so it stays on.

### 5.2 Boxes, buttons and doors

5. **The Clover corridor** (box twist). A one-tile corridor: start, box, floor, button, floor, floor, exit, where the button opens the exit. Carrying the box to the button and putting it down from the start side seals the corridor with the exit open beyond it; the answer is to carry the box over the button, turn on the far side (turn key) and put it down facing back. _From:_ Stephen's Sausage Roll's _The Clover_. _Teaches:_ a box bars its tile; the put-down tile is the one faced. _Check:_ guideline 1 (button not next to the exit), guideline 9; it's fully reversible, which is why it suits a game with no undo.
6. **Hands full** (box test). The button sits in a room reached only by portal. Carrying the box, the subject can't shoot, so it must shoot first, or put the box down, shoot and pick it up again. Escalation: the only tile that sees the right wall is one the subject reaches while carrying. _From:_ the carry rule plus Snakebird's "too short, too long". _Check:_ guideline 18 (a tile to put the box down on wherever the subject can arrive carrying it).
7. **One box, two buttons in turn** (box combine). One box must hold button A (opens door A) and later button B (bridges a ditch or opens door B); taking it off A closes door A behind the subject, so the order and a portal placed while A was held decide the route. _From:_ Chip's _Teleblock_; for Jarušek and Pelánek's decomposition finding, the route must also go back to the box after placing a portal while A is held (measure it as in 3.4). _Teaches:_ nothing new; it's a test of planning. _Check:_ guideline 7 (box starts not on tiles where it could land under the subject), guideline 1.
8. **Counterintuitive first move** (box twist). The box starts beside its button, but putting it there first blocks the corridor to the only wall the subject needs to shoot. The answer carries it away, shoots, then comes back. _From:_ the counterintuitive-move metric. _Check:_ guideline 9 (a reachable tile beside the box wherever it rests).
9. **Prepare, then trigger** (switch combine). The exit opens only by a switch whose links also send both boxes home, so the buttons they held come up and their doors close; the player must have placed portals beforehand so the route to the exit survives. The antlines show what the switch will do. _From:_ Lolo's sleeping monsters. _Check:_ guideline 10 (switch, not button, sends boxes home), guideline 6 (box start tiles), guideline 7.

### 5.3 Fizzlers

10. **Set your portals first** (fizzler test). Box on one side of a fizzler that is on, button on the other. Walking the box through destroys it and closes both portals; the answer is to place both portals from a tile whose shot lines miss the field, then carry the box through the portals. _From:_ Portal TC09. _Check:_ guideline 13 (don't make the player stand in the field to aim), guideline 12 (the walls behind the connectors take no portals).
11. **Fizzle to respawn** (fizzler twist). The box starts on button B1, which holds door D1 open. The route needs the box elsewhere first (taking it closes D1), and later needs D1 open again from a side where B1 can't be reached (past an arrow). The answer: carry the box into the fizzler; it flies home onto B1 and D1 reopens. _From:_ the Portal 2 community's "fizzle to respawn"; Baba's _Tiny Pond_. _Teaches:_ a destroyed box goes to its start, wherever that is. _Check:_ guideline 6 (this is its "box delivered by its start tile" used on purpose), guideline 7 (the subject is never on B1 when the box lands), an antline from B1 to D1 so the effect reads.
12. **Cheap first failure** (fizzler teach). The obvious walk carries the box straight into a fizzler that is on: it breaks up and lands back at its start two tiles away, and both portals close. Nothing is lost but a few steps, and the way round (a portal pair placed first) is then plain. _From:_ Chip's _Tiny_ (an instant, cheap failure in a tiny room), Birdwell ("see other characters make mistakes"). A box can't _start_ on a fizzler, a door or a laser, so in Portal 2D the demonstration has to be one the player causes. _Check:_ guideline 5 (the start tile stays in reach), guideline 13.

### 5.4 Light barriers

13. **A wall you can switch** (light barrier teach). A room split by a light barrier run held off by a button and its box; the barrier blocks walking and shots, so neither the subject nor a shot crosses until the box is on the button. _From:_ first contact forced and safe. _Check:_ guideline 1 (button not next to the band), guideline 2.
14. **Barrier as a stopper** (slide and light barrier combine). A slide lane overshoots the tile beside the exit; a light barrier across the lane, held off by a receiver lit by a ray, comes on when the player puts a box in the ray, and the next slide stops in front of the band. _From:_ the Lara Croft GO expansion's eyes; Portal 2's _Turret Blocker_. Close to your own slide-bridge idea in `level_design/levels.txt`. _Check:_ guideline 30 (the ray must not cross the barrier's tile), guideline 20 (work out every stop with the band on and off), guideline 31.

### 5.5 Ditches and blocked walls

15. **Ferry across islands** (portal and box combine). Islands separated by open ditches, crossed only by portals; the box must reach the last island. Each hop is put down, re-aim, pick up, cross. _From:_ Chip's _Oversea Delivery_. _Check:_ guideline 18 (a tile to put the box down on each island), guideline 17 (a way back from each island while it matters).
16. **Pull the rug** (ditch twist). A box rests on a bridged ditch, held by a button; releasing the button destroys the box, which flies home to a start tile beyond a barrier the subject can't carry it past. _From:_ Portal 2's _Pull The Rug_. _Check:_ guideline 1 (never under the subject), guideline 6 (the start tile is meant to be reached this way).
17. **Steel that opens** (blocked wall teach). The only wall that sees across the gap is a blocked wall, which takes portals only while a button holds it; the box goes on the button, the portal goes on the wall, the subject crosses. _From:_ Portal's staged tool; Quantum Conundrum's per-room battery. _Teaches:_ blocked walls refuse portals until a link opens them. _Check:_ guideline 2 (no side of the linked blocked wall reachable by crossing from the button), guideline 13 (its portal vanishes if the wall is blocked again).
18. **The portal budget** (blocked wall combine). One switch unblocks a group of walls, changing which faces take portals across the whole chamber; the player must choose when to use it, since switches can't be undone. _From:_ Ocarina's Water Temple via Boss Keys. _Check:_ guideline 4 (only the switch links those walls).

### 5.6 Slide floors (beyond chambers 4 and 5 and 04a's ideas)

19. **Box as a stopper** (slide twist). A long lane ends at a dead end; the exit (or a side door) is one tile before the end. Without the box the subject overshoots; with the box on the end tile it stops beside the door. Escalation: the only box is also needed on a button, so a portal has to take over one of its jobs. _From:_ Chip's blocks on ice; Ricochet's helper robots. _Check:_ guideline 20, guideline 24, and Kluiver's caution that free boxes make most of an open field reachable.
20. **Nine-square lattice** (slide and portal combine). A 3×3 lattice of plain stop tiles joined by slide lanes, some walled off; walls at lane ends take portals, so the player edits the graph. _From:_ Chip's _I Slide_; switch stations from _Blink_. _Check:_ guideline 24 (every stop tile has a move that leads somewhere new), guideline 25 (no portal loop by surprise), guideline 26.

### 5.7 Turnable arrows (beyond chambers 6 and 7 and 04a's ideas)

21. **The arrow as a firing post** (arrow twist). The only tile that sees the wall the route needs is an arrow; the subject can face only the arrow's way there, so it has to turn the arrow with its button or switch to aim. _From:_ Portal 2D's own rules; Lolo's arrows don't stop shots either. _Check:_ guideline 16 for both ways the arrow points, guideline 27 (the player sees it turn first).
22. **Funnel, then flip** (arrow combine). A room entered through a fixed arrow; inside, a turnable arrow held by a button opens the way back only while held, so the box has to stay on the button, and the route must not need it afterwards. _From:_ Lolo. _Check:_ guideline 28 (reach the turnable arrow from plain floor), guideline 29, guideline 5 (no box needed beyond the valve).

### 5.8 Rays and mirror boxes (beyond chambers 8 and 9 and 04a's ideas)

23. **Mirror box shown in place** (mirror box teach). The ray hits a mirror box resting one tile off, which turns it into a wall; moving the box one tile turns it onto the receiver. _From:_ Q.U.B.E. 2. _Check:_ guideline 31 (every place the box can rest), guideline 19 (no switch where the subject stands to put it down).
24. **Mirror first, then needed elsewhere** (mirror box test). The mirror box lights the receiver that opens door 1, and then is needed on the button that opens door 2; the portal pair has to take over the ray. _From:_ Portal 2's Laser Stairs. _Check:_ guideline 31, guideline 30.
25. **Two jobs at the bend** (mirror box twist). The same as 24, except a button sits on the ray's corner tile: one mirror box can turn the ray and hold the button at once. Make another corner look like the obvious one. _From:_ the Talos tripod needed twice. _Check:_ rays pass buttons and a resting mirror box presses one (`docs/elements.md`); keep only one box in the chamber.
26. **Toggle between two receivers** (mirror box twist). Without the mirror box the ray reaches receiver A (door 1); with it, receiver B (door 2). The route needs both doors in turn, so the box goes down, comes up and goes down again. _From:_ Zelda's crystal switch (via Boss Keys), rebuilt. _Check:_ guideline 30 for both rays, guideline 31.
27. **A portal keeps what a door gave briefly** (ray and door combine). While a button holds door D open, the only line to wall W runs through it: put the box on the button, shoot a portal onto W, take the box to a second button and let D close. A portal in the ray then sends it out beyond D into the receiver. _From:_ the Talos connector puzzle, rebuilt. _Check:_ W isn't the tile right behind D; guideline 30; what the subject itself can reach through the same portal pair (guideline 14).
28. **Relay with a detour** (ray twist). Receiver R1 switches on emitter E2, whose ray opens the exit through R2; the subject's only route crosses the first ray, which would darken the whole chain. The answer routes the first ray through a portal pair so it skips the route. _From:_ Talos connectors; Laser Maze checkpoints. _Check:_ guideline 30 (no ray crosses what its own receiver holds), guideline 4 (one receiver per element); the macro solver refuses chambers with receivers, so check by hand (guideline 38).
29. **A run of four** (mirror box sequence across 3–4 chambers). One mirror box turns the ray onto a receiver; then the corner tile is a button; then two receivers; then a chamber that looks like the third but needs the portal pair instead, because the mirror box is needed on a button out of line. _From:_ Blow's partition run, which slips in the piece its last panel turns on two panels earlier: so let an earlier chamber of the four use the portal pair to carry the ray where nothing depends on it. _Check:_ each chamber on its own as above; the fourth is the one that breaks the habit.

### 5.9 Combine across elements

30. **Filters** (combine, late). One chamber where the subject, the box, a shot and the ray each reach a different region: an open ditch passes rays and shots but not the subject; a fizzler passes the subject but not the box or a shot; a light barrier passes nothing. The puzzle is ferrying each across its own barrier. _From:_ Talos 2's driller and exclusion field. _Check:_ guideline 22 (if slides are involved), guideline 30, and the solver's refusal of chambers with receivers.
31. **The capstone** (finale). Portal's TC18 introduces nothing and combines nearly everything; Valve's finale was "about the easiest puzzle in Portal". Without time pressure, Portal 2D's last chamber needs synthesis and spectacle: several elements working at once, a visible chain of doors with lit antlines, and a catch no harder than the run before it.
